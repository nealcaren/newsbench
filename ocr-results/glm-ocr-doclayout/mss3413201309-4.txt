The Maine Association Opposed to Suffrage for Women speaks for the majority of the women of Maine. This the suffragists in effect admitted when they failed to request the legislature to provide for a referendum by the women of this State on the suffragist question.

Theoretical objections to such a referendum may be offered from both sides. The antis may object that, if every woman in Maine were to declare for woman suffrage, the objections to such a policy, arising from consideration of the limitations Nature has imposed upon her functions, and from consideration of her natural and proper relation to the family, society and the state, would remain unaffected and in full force.

So also the suffragists may object that the suffrage is a natural right, wherefore it belongs to every man, woman or child whether he or she chooses to use it wisely, unwisely or not at all, and without regard to whether the women in the state who wish to get into politics are many or few.

Neither side could consent to leave the question to the decision of a referendum by the women of the state without surrendering theoretical objections to such a course.

Ours is neither a despotism nor a pure democracy, but a representative government. And a representative government is one in which a portion of the people represents all the people. It is one in which qualifications attach to the suffrage.

If it is to be a truly representative government, all classes and interests must be represented, each in proportion to its strength. Whether despotism, involving the abolition of popular elections, would or would not be less wasteful, it at least would not be representative government.

Females are not a class, but are members of all classes. Therefore woman suffrage is not a necessary part of representative government. The ideal representative government is one containing the smallest number of electors consistent with its purpose.

Women as a sex have no interest in government apart from that of men. Therefore democracy is not broadened by enfranchising women.

It is fortunate for woman that her interest in government is identical with man's. If the contrary were true, the diverse and selfish interest of the stronger sex would eventually result in the denial of justice to woman precisely as the negro has suffered this denial in the South, though armed with the ballot.

(FormerAttorney General of the U. S.)
The American man in time of peace is an easy-going, trouble-avoiding creature. Especially in his dealings with women, he seems to have laid to heart Shakespeare's admonition:
"In peace there's nothing so becomes a man,
As meekness and humility."

The common answer given by the average man who declares himself in favor of woman suffrage is, "Oh, well, the women want it and we might as well let them have it." Whether or not it is good for them; or still more important, whether or not it is to the interest of the commonwealth that our already complex electorate should be further complicated by remale suffrage, is brushed aside as a matter of small moment. Yet I seriously question whether anything like a majority of the men of any American community are in favor of giving women the vote. There are many reasons against it. Some are obvious. It is difficult to discuss the question frankly. Most men feel a hesitancy in discussing in a mixed assemblage the fundamental questions of sex that radically differentate women from men. The divine privilege of motherhood would seem to me to be of far higher import than the vote, and I am one of those who still think of women as the object of love, adoration, of reverence, whether as sweethearts, wives or mothers, and therefore, the thought of them engaged in electioneering, haranguing crowds from wagons or soap boxes as one sees many women nowadays, is repugnant to all my ideas of womanhood.

Despite the clamorous claims of the advocates of votes for women, there is no preponderance of evidence that the communities in which they have secured that privilege are any better for it. No wise laws for the protection of women and children have been enacted in the suffrage States that are not to be found on the statute books of New York. It may be seriously questioned whether in States where they have no vote women do not possess more real influence for good than in States where they have the suffrage. Certainly no woman in this State can with justice complain that its laws or their administration discriminate against her or the interests of her sex.

Where, three years ago, I said to the woman who wished to take hold of the wheel of the Ship of State, "Yes!" now I say to her "No!" This, from now on, is a man's job. Gentleness, love and mercy are needed, but they are no longer in place on the bridge; there there is room only for resolution and force.

Today the world's every Ship of State is in distress; on the bridge, and at every important station there must be men in control. Women have their part, but it is with the immensely important work below decks. Theirs, indubitably, is not the function of government, or direction in the hour of danger.

This has suddenly become a masculine age. It calls for the uttermost assertion of the martial instincts of mankind. To be strong is to prevail; to be weak is to invite defeat, to suffer national disaster, the woman with the man. The nation that in this emergency permits women to usurp the governmental functions of men will rue the day that it exchanged masculine strength for feminine weakness.

The determined stand that the suffrage leaders, one and all, have taken to defeat our preparations for defense and to compel us to accept, in the guise of peace, degradation at the hands of Germany is an augury of where we as a nation will end if the political power which these leaders are trying to grasp is finally put into their hands.

Never was there a more eloquent confession of woman's inability to support the strains of a war council than that so pathetically made by Miss Rankin in the moment of national crisis, when, in her agony, she cried out, "I want to stand by my country, but I cannot vote for war!" And again, when on April 25th, in reply to a demand from her constituents that she support the President's selective conscription measure passed but three days later by an enormous majority, she wired: "I have heard of no one in the House who is supporting the President's conscription bill. Do you wish me to stand alone for this?"

SALOON EVIL
IS DESTROYED

Men Voters Have Banished It Without Aid of Women

The suffragists have been assuring the public for years that woman suffrage would do away with saloons. Do the facts support this statement? NEVADA gave women the vote in 1914. At the first election in Reno at which women could vote the majority of the women who went to th polls voted against a reduction of the number of saloons in that city, and were publicly thanked by the liquor men. Nevada is totally black on the wet and dry map. The only two white spots are the Indian Reservations, where the Federal Government will not allow the sale of liquor.

COLORADO defeated Prohibition by 50,000 after women had voted nearly twenty years. It was finally carried by only 10,000 majority.

CALIFORNIA in 1914 defeated Prohibition by a majority of nearly 200,000. It is everywhere admitted that the women voted overwhelmingly against it. In 1916 two Prohibition bills were submitted to the people one for complete Prohibition, the other for a restrictive sale measure and both were defeated.

The saloon and dance hall situation in San Francisco has long been notorious. Indeed it has become so intolerable that a popular uprising in February has affected an at least temporary suppression of the most flagrant offenders,—and this not by the use of the ballot but by the force of aroused Public Opinion.

In ILLINOIS the strongest claim which the head of the Anti-saloon league, although an ardent suffragist, can make is that the drys "held their own" in Illinois in 1916. In Chicago 23 out of 27 aldermen elected in 1916 were those endorsed by the saloons. In East Dubuque, Ill., 74 per cent of the women who voted cast their votes for the saloons.

Two states which adjoin Illinois (Iowa and Michigan) are dry, under male suffrage. In MASSACHUSETTS the drys much more than "held their own" in the 1916 elections. Besides increasing their majorities throughout the state they gained six cities—Fall River, Haverhill, Lawrence, North Adams, Fitchburg and Leominster. Cambridge, with a population of more than 100,000, has been dry thirty years under male suffrage.

The drys are gaining rapidly in many states where women do not vote. In Illinois, on the other hand, the officers of the Anti-saloon League told several audiences during the last campaign that their state (where women vote!) could not hope for prohibition except by National Amendment.

More than ONE-HALF of the counties of Minnesota are "dry" by local option and there is an amendment to be submitted in the next general election which will undoubtedly put Minnesota in the "dry" column. A REFLECTION UPON MEN.

The demand for "Votes for Women" casts an unjustifiable reflection upon the men of Maine.

It carries with it the insinuation that men have wilfully neglected to protect the rights of women.

This insinuation is both unjust and ungenerous.

The creed of the men of Maine, as of all the civilized world, is "Women and Children First," on land as well as on sea.

In countless ways the laws of Maine give precedence to women in property rights and obligations.

In their recognition of the rights of women, the laws of male-suffrage Maine are far in advance of those of any state where women enjoy the franchise.

Every suffragist, by demanding the vote, practically declares that the fathers, husbands, sons and brothers of Maine are not to be trusted by their wives, mothers, sisters and daughters.

Every opponent of woman suffrage repudiates this baseless allegation. Speaking for at least 80 per cent. of the women of Maine, we ask the men of Maine to believe that we have not lost our faith in them. And we ask them to assure us of that belief by VOTING "NO" ON WOMAN SUF FRAGE Sept. 10.

Woman Suffrage wins only when its opponents neglect to vote. Don't fail to vote NO Sept. 10.

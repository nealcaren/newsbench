THE HOUSE WHERE WASHINGTON SLEPT

Tradition Dots the Whole Eastern Landscape With It, But Rhode Island Has a Few That Are Well Authenticated

WOODROW Wilson has set the fashion in telling the truth about Washington. As a historian unswayed by emotion and kindred feelings, he has gone be- mind the romance which has so completely enveloped the character of our first President and drawn a picture of the man that has been altogether too realistic for those worshippers who like halos with their heroes in history.

As of the man, so of his movements has a deal of untruth been uttered. Rhode Island has contributed its share. All over the State, as all up and down the Atlantic coast, are houses in which it is valiantly asserted Washington once slept. Wherever one finds a house with the date of erection preceding the Revolution, one may confidently expect to hear that at such and such a time Washington was there and stayed through the night.

But the facts do not make him out such a continuous and persistent sleeper. When the mists of tradition are brushed away, the record shows that there were just four places in the State, in which it is certain that Washington spent some time as a guest. Three were in Providence and one in East Greenwich. All claims of the rest to fame are of dubious foundation.

Although Washington stayed several days on each of his visits to Newport, there is nothing to indicate where he rested o' night. That he spread his time as generously and as conveniently as he was able among several of the leading families there is little question. But neither he nor contemporary writers give any names; and to-day in Newport only Trinity Church claims him as its own.

Whether or not he went to church while in the town by the sea is a problem that may never be solved satisfactorily. The tradition of his visit to Trinity and of the pew he occupied has been paraded and then seemingly punctured so many times that any arbitrary attempt to settle it would revive a controversy that is better quiescent. It may be said in all fairness, however that the modern demand for historical fact regardless of romance has had its effect in the case of old Trinity and its cherished memory of the past.

The visit was made in the Hopkins homestead on Hopkins street and when the house was marked with a tablet four years ago the event was duly chronicled on the bronze.

After that brief sojourn, the exigencies of war prevented Washington from setting foot in this State again until March, 1781. On Tuesday, March 6, of that year, he arrived in Newport, with his personal staff to visit the French General, Count Rochambeau, and to take a few days' rest before going South where Gen. Nathanael Greene was harrassing the British and preparing the way for the campaign that was not only to clip Cornwallis' wings, but also to end the war.

On reaching Newport he was met at the Jamestown ferry by a barge from the French flagship and taken aboard for dinner with the French Admiral. With him went Rochambeau and other French officers. There was a short discussion of plans for the utilizing of the French fleet in the campaign at hand and then the Commander-in-Chief and Rochambeau came ashore. All Newport was there to greet him.

He made his landing at Long Wharf and marched with Rochambeau to the latter's headquarters through two long lines of soldiers. Behind the soldiers stood the citizens packed deep in rows. The whole town closed up for the reception. Contemporary chronicles speak of many "loud huzzag" for Washington and the great delight of one and all at being able to welcome such a distinguished visitor to the town.

"When Gen. Washington landed," wrote a reporter for the Providence Gazette, "in consequence of orders from the Court of France, he was received with all the honors ever paid to the Princes Royal, or a Marshal of France, greater than which can be rendered only to the King."

Where Washington spent the night is not certain, but it was probably with Rochambeau. They were together after the grand illumination in Washington's honor and it is likely that they returned to Rochambeau's quarters, where they talked well into the night. The next day was also spent in private discussion. The conference with Rochambeau lasted well into the afternoon, halting only when it became imperative to adjourn that everybody might get ready for the ball that was given for the town's guests in Mrs. Cawley's assembly room. Washington had as his partner for the first dance, Miss Margaret Champlin, one of Newport's belles. He undoubtedly danced until the small hours of the morning.

He remained in Newport until the following Tuesday—a whole week to be exact. He attended various dinners and went to various dances. But whose guest he was through the week history does not record. Metcalf Bowler gave a dinner in his honor at the Bowler farm in Portsmouth and he may have visited there a day or two. There used to be an old house in Portsmouth not far from the original Bowler estate, that was for years pointed out as one in which "Washington slept." The tradition has been handed down through the years, but its authenticity has never been proved.

On Tuesday, March 13. Washington with Maj. Gen. Howe and his officers came overland to Providence. He was met at the lower ferry by a delegation of citizens and escorted to the house of Jabez Bowen, then one of the prominent citizens of the town. The house stood near the foot of what is now College street, the right of way to which was sold to the college on the hill by another Jabez Bowen in 1798.

On his way to the Bowen house he was saluted by 13 guns fired by the members of the Continental Park Artillery and also by individual salutes from the vessels lying in the harbor, each of which was decorated with flags and bunting. Providence was then a large shipping centre and as all the Indiamen tied up at the wharves carried guns, there was plenty of noise along the water front. "Likewise," said the truthful Gazette, "he was welcomed by the cheerful countenances of the inhabitants."

That night the town and the vessels in the harbor were "beautifully illuminated." The next day Washington and his staff inspected the town, then went to the old State House, where an "elegant entertainment had been provided." Coming directly ashore, Washington and his friends went to the Golden Ball Inn, which later became the Mansion House on Benefit street. The inn had been built and opened in 1783 by Henry Rice, who straightway made it a hostelry second to none in New England. For years it was the centre of much of the town's social activity. Until the coming of the City Hotel it was the town's only public house.

WARD 1 SUFFRAGISTS MEET.
Members of New District 1 Oraganiza tion Plan Canvass.
Members of the new Woman Suffrage party of Providence in Ward 1, District 1, held their first regular meeting last evening at the home of Mrs. Elizabeth Monks, 159 Benefit street. A definite plan was adopted for a regular canvass of the district and the spreading of woman suffrage propaganda.

At the opening of the meeting, which was well attended, Miss Helen Thomas was elected captain in the district, to have direction of the details of the work for the cause. A discussion of the methods used by suffrage workers in New York, Chicago and other cities followed, after which the members mapped out plans for a similar campaign here.

The first thing that Washington did after the dying out of the excitement attendant upon his arrival was to visit the college grounds in company with Dr. Manning. In the evening he also went to the campus to view the illuminations. Next morning he showed his interest in the town's principal industry by going down to one of the shipyards and viewing one of Brown & Ives's big Indiamen then in the stocks. In the afternoon he and his party were guests at an entertainment and reception held in the Court House. Again 13 toasts were drunk and again the President remarked upon the hospitality of his friends in Providence. "It may be proper to remark," added the Gazette, "that no untoward accident took place, that every countenance indicated the most heartfelt joy and that we have reason to believe the President was perfectly satisfied with his reception."

The following morning Washington set sail in the Hancock for New York, never again to honor either Providence or Rhode Island by a visit. There were two occasions when Washington touched Rhode Island soil that he did not come to Providence. Both times he was the guest of his old comrade in arms, Gen. James M. Varnum, who built the Varnum mansion in East Greenwich overlooking Cowesset bay. Gen. Varnum was a jolly soul who loved good things and who was noted for the punch that he brewed.

Once when Washington was on a visit to Norwich, Conn., he made a detour in order to greet Gen. Varnum and to sample some of the Varnum punch. At another time when he was on his way to Labanon, also in Connecticut, he stopped over for a chat with the General. He slept in the state bedroom on the second floor of the great house. Here, also, Lafayette was a guest when he came to Rhode Island.

There is one interesting feature in connection with Washington's visits to this State. In none of them was any mention ever made of his body-servant, Cato, who enjoyed an annual death for 50 years and more. There have been nearly as many Catos as there are houses where Washington slept. But no native chronicler ever took the trouble to speak of the original negro and nobody, not even the most ardent patrons of the Golden Ball Inn, ever asserted that Cato graced that hostelry with his presence.

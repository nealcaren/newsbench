Ernest Hogan, the author-actor, who is with the Black Patti Troubadours, has not a drop of white blood in his veins, and so the story of his life and success is especially interesting to those studying the rise of the African. He is the most thorough representative of his people, for his skin is so dark that his eyes are only brown in comparison.

He has the high, broad forehead seen so often among the Anglo-Saxons, and a loftiness of top head that is rare. When he is in repose there is a sadness about it infinitely touching when in the black face it is so rare. It is only occasionally, in spite of the blackness of him, that it occurred to me, in conversation, that I was not talking to an Anglo-Saxon, so free is he from any trace of the peculiarities of his people. Occasionally when he laughs with the chuckle and keen enjoyment known only in the Negro, then rolls his eyes a bit, he is similar to his own race.

He has the peculiarly melodious voice which all Negroes possess, and although he is a comedian, he never lets away from the South, he occasionally falls back into the quaint rhythmical accent so characteristic of the Southern black man.

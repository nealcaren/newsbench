THE FINE TREE.

O lift the stately emblem on the Bay State's
rusted shield,
Give to Northern winds the Fine Tree on our
banner's field!
Sons of men, who sit in council with their Bibles,
Answering England's royal missive with a firm, 'Twas
Raised in strife for home and freedom—set the battle in
array!'
What are gold and silver worth to thee and me?
Has the old tree stood so long, and shall it die?
Free as our rivers are
Ocean-wand pine—
Free as the winds that blow,
Free as the snow that's blowing.
Up, ye sons of men, then,
Haste ye, and summon
Courage and loveliness,
Manhood and woman!
Deplete our pedigree by
The blood of Andros down—
For another strong-viced Adam in the city's streets to
try!

Oh, ye God and Massachusetts—set your feet on
the land!
Perish gold and perish traffic—spurn your fetter's
hand!
But, in Heaven's name, keep your honor—keep the
heart of the Bay State sound!
Where's the man for liberty? Where's the man
for right?
Where's the man to light up bonfires from her
mountains to the sea?
Beats her Pilgrim pulse no longer? Sit she dumb in
her despair?
Has she lost the spirit of her fathers? Has she none
to dare?
Oh, my God! for one right worthy to lift up her-
sons' shield,
And to plant again the Fine Tree in her banner's
tattooed field!

THE ANTI-SLAVERY OF NEW ENGLAND. A Sermon
preached at the opening of the Essex County Anti-
Slavery Convention, Haverhill, Mass., Sept. 20, 1855. By Charles
Burleigh. Boston: Anti-Slavery Society. 12mo. pp. 35.

This pamphlet shows all the expectancy, but less
of the ability, of a Beecher. Commencing with the
declaration that the Bible is 'unquestionably, the
strongest anti-slavery document in the world,' he
proceeds, in addition to this, to show that the
hypocrisy constantly assumed by the Orthodox
school is a fact—namely, that that collection of books
is infallibly inspired of God—and it then takes the
further position of denying the inspiration of the
Bible, and of asserting that its morality is common
sense, or that
cannot be defended as honorable and right.

What of 'The Antislavery of New England' referred
to in this pamphlet, remains somewhat obscure, in
spite of the thirty-five pages that are occupied with
describing, satirizing, and denouncing it. It is a
'criticism,' says the writer, 'of the clerical hypothesis
above mentioned, or demands of efficient evidence in
support of it.'

The author's main argument is that the author-
ship of the Bible is a matter of 'common sense,' and
that the only way to defend it is to show that the
'criticism' is a fact—namely, that that collection of books
is infallibly inspired of God—and it then takes the

Salutary

The Maine Association Opposed to Suffrage for Women speaks for the majority of the women of Maine. This the suffragists in effect admitted when they failed to request the legislature to provide for a referendum by the women of this State on the suffragist question.

Theoretical objections to such a referendum may be offered from both sides. The antis may object that, if every woman in Maine were to declare for woman suffrage, the objections to such a policy, arising from consideration of the limitations Nature has imposed upon her functions, and from consideration of her natural and proper relation to the family, society and the state, would remain unaffected and in full force.

So also the suffragists may object that the suffrage is a natural right, wherefore it belongs to every man, woman or child whether he or she chooses to use it wisely, unwisely or not at all, and without regard to whether the women in the state who wish to get into politics are many or few.

Neither side could consent to leave the question to the decision of a referendum by the women of the state without surrendering theoretical objections to such a course.

The fact remains, however, that either side is practical enough to favor any method of decision which would help it towards its goal, and that the practical objection by the suffragists to a referendum by the women is their knowledge that it would

Hence it is that the women composing the various anti-suffrage organizations of Maine have felt obliged to come before the public at this time and acquaint the voters with the real sentiments of most women in the state. This duty performed, they must leave the responsibility for the decision to the men of Maine, but not without insisting upon the immense importance of the special election September 10th, next, not without insisting that the opponent of woman suffrage who fails to go to the polls September 10 and cast his ballot will be recreant to the responsibility this state has imposed upon him.

Remember that wherever woman suffrage comes it comes to stay. The solitary exception is New Jersey, which adopted woman suffrage as early as 1777 and abandoned it after a trial extending to 1844 which proved its futility and wastefulness.

In every one of the eleven states, except Mormon Utah, in which woman suffrage has been placed in the constitution, the women in the majority have repealed it automatically by failing to exercise it; but fear of the women politicians has prevented any attempt to get rid of it formally and legally. Experience has shown that the easiest method of avoiding it is to vote it down at the polls when it is proposed to the voters in the form of an amendment to the constitution, as it will be to the voters

Ours is neither a despotism nor a pure democracy, but a representative government. And a representative government is one in which a portion of the people represents all the people. It is one in which qualifications attach to the suffrage.

If it is to be a truly representative government, all classes and interests must be represented, each in proportion to its strength. Whether despotism, involving the abolition of popular elections, would or would not be less wasteful, it at least would not be representative government.

Females are not a class, but are members of all classes. Therefore woman suffrage is not a necessary part of representative government. The ideal representative government is one containing the smallest number of electors consistent with its purpose.

Women as a sex have no interest in government apart from that of men. Therefore democracy is not broadened by enfranchising women.

It is fortunate for woman that her interest in government is identical with man's. If the contrary were true, the diverse and selfish interest of the stronger sex would eventually result in the denial of justice to woman precisely as the negro has suffered this denial in the South, though armed with the ballot.

WOMAN SUFFRAGE ESSENTIAL TO SOCIALISM.

That the extension of the franchise to women is essential to the success of Socialism is admitted by every Socialist leader. The New York "Call," a Socialist organ says:

"The fight for woman suffrage is bound to be an exceptionally hard one, but it is a fight that must be won, and on none does the responsibility for success fall more heavily than upon the Socialists. And the time for Socialists to begin their great campaign to which they are morally bound is now. Socialists, remember that the mobilizing for the woman suffrage fight takes place today."

The National Woman Suffrage Association is distributing Socialist literature and employing Socialist speakers, and Socialism is rampant in every state and country where women have the ballot.

Do you wish to advance the cause of Socialism? If not vote "No" on Woman Suffrage September 10.

AN ADDRESS BY HON. GEORGE W. WICKERSHAM

(Former Attorney General of the U.S.)

The American man in time of peace is an easy-going, trouble-avoiding creature. Especially in his dealings with women, he seems to have laid to heart Shakespeare's admonition:

"In peace there's nothing so becomes a man,

As meekness and humility."

The common answer given by the average man who declares himself in favor of woman suffrage is, "Oh, well, the women want it and we might as well let them have it." Whether or not it is good for them; or still more important, whether or not it is to the interest of the commonwealth that our already complex electorate should be further complicated by male suffrage, is brushed aside as a matter of small moment. Yet I seriously question whether anything like a majority of the men of any American community are in favor of giving women the vote. There are many reasons against it. Some are obvious. It is difficult to discuss the question frankly. Most men feel a hesitancy in discussing in a mixed assemblage the fundamental questions of sex that radically differentiate women from men. The divine privilege of motherhood would seem to me to be of far higher import than the vote, and I am one of those who still think of women as the object of love, adoration, of reverence, whether as sweethearts, wives or mothers, and therefore, the thought of them engaged in engineering, haranguing crowds from wagons or soap boxes as one sees many women nowadays, is repugnant to all my ideas of womanhood.

Despite the clamorous claims of the advocates of votes for women, there is no preponderance of evidence that the communities in which they have secured that privilege are any better for it. No wise laws for the protection of women and children have been enacted in the suffrage States that are not to be found on the statute books of New York. It may be seriously questioned whether in States where they have no vote women do not possess more real influence for good than in States where they have the suffrage. Certainly no woman in this State can with justice complain that its laws or their administration discriminate against her or the interests of her sex.

In France a woman never seems to forget that she is the first of all a woman, and her mission in these later years seems to have been to make France and the world realize that next to being a fighting man, there is nothing more glorious than womanhood, and that the supreme glory consists in being the mother of a son who gives his life for his country. Cannot the women of America emulate their sisters beyond the sea and learn that not by intruding upon man's estate is their sex to be glorified, but rather by making of their womanhood that holy thing that for centuries has held mankind in thraldom to them.

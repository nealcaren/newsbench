Industrial Equality

Ella M. Sherwin Speaks Before  
League of Women Voters

Monday, March 12, the League of Women Voters of New York City held its annual convention at Hotel Pennsylvania. Mrs. Ada R. Wolff had been asked to speak on Industrial Equality principles, but was unable to do so because of a previous engagement, and Miss Ella M. Sherwin, corresponding secretary of the Women’s Equal Opportunity League, took up the verbal cudgels in defense of our firm stand against so-called protective legislation.

Miss Sherwin is a member of the League of Women Voters of Brooklyn and her uncompromising attitude against 8-hour and minimum wage bills for women is concurred in by many other women who are members of the Women Voters League, in this city and elsewhere.

Miss Sherwin’s speech was listened to with much interest by her audience, and after the meeting many women told her they believed she was right.

Mrs. Frances Perkins, Industrial Commissioner, spoke in the affirmative, but, as she herself said, recently, all her arguments and pleas were for the “happier” industry and we have always contended that the he-happer and the she-happer should be prevented, through restrictive legislation, from leaving school too soon and from entering industry in competition with adults. A forty-eight-hour law for minors of both sexes up to 18 would be a proper exercise of the State’s authority over its wards, and a minimum wage law for such minors would discourage their employment.

The League passed a resolution endorsing the eight-hour and minimum wage bills now pending in New York State Legislature—said resolution being passed BEFORE Miss Sherwin spoke. Verbum satis sapienti.

EQUALITY BEFORE THE EMPLOYER IS THE SLEEPING-STONE TO ALL HUMAN RIGHTS

Speech of Irene Paquet Before the Providence League of Women Voters, March 20, 1923

What the wage-earning woman needs is full equality with wage-earning men—and as any woman is liable to be “thrown on her own” at any time, by an act of Nature or the cussedness of human nature, wage-earning women means any woman, from the highest to the lowest in social position, from the richest to the poorest in this world’s goods.

Those who advocate special legislation for women in industry are, strangely enough, quite certain that women need and must have political equality in civil law. While claiming that woman is able to exercise the fullest control over her children, her home, her business, if she owns it herself, her bankbook, her vote, her hours spent in political spellbinding, these illogicians say that as soon as she goes into the factory to make for pay what her grandmother was obliged to make for only her board and keep, in the past, as soon as she goes into the store to sell—for pay—what her grandmother sold in barter to itinerant peddlers, then woman suffers a change in her status and becomes a subject for laws and regulations that make her of no more value to her employer than a child under sixteen and forever preclude her from receiving equal pay since she is forbidden by law to perform “equal work.”

After an intensive investigation extending over many months the National Woman’s Party has arrived at this unanimous conclusion: All the laws purporting to be in favor of women are in PRACTICE, if not in theory, ONE OF THE WEAPONS TO EXPLOIT WOMEN.

In the District of Columbia, which has an eight-hour law (this does not forbid night-shift work, however) and a minimum wage law for women and minors, women have been replaced by men in many occupations, because employers can work the men for longer hours at overtime rates, while the woman must stop on the hour or the employer will be fined. So men, and boys over 16, get the jobs; and this is the case in other states having such laws, and will be the case in your state if the Lavender eight-hour bill for minors and women becomes law.

An eight-hour day by arrangement between the employer and his employees, such as many of your mills and stores now have, and which applies to all the employees without sex distinction, is the only fair kind of hour regulation, and if the labor leaders were on the level that is the only kind they would desire; but those of you who attended the hearings at the State House and listened to these men plead for a bill putting women on an equal footing with children, at the same time bitterly attacking the bill aiming to give men and women the same desirable working hours, can have no illusions as to what these men really want: the industrial subjection of women; high wages for themselves based on the lowered wages of the women, and all the overtime, at time and a half rates, denied to the women by law.

Arouse yourselves to the danger of such laws—women must no longer be the regulated half of humanity. Because we are mothers, actual and potential, we must be free and able to earn as much as man, when necessary, in order to give our children the proper food, education and environment. Nature intended us to do this and has made us strong and given us an endurance not possessed by men in order that we shall be able to bring our children to maturity even without the aid of the often transitory and sometimes questionable migratory male; look about you and see the proof of this assertion in the many women of your acquaintance who have done—and done well—what welfare is impossible and seek to make impossible by law. And remember every one of these restrictive laws creates a bureau of enforcement, paid for out of your pocket; inspectors and commissioners to poke their noses into your home and your way of providing for and bringing up your children—and you are taxed to pay the wages of these inspectors and commissioners and clerks, although your earning power has been curtailed, often lost, through the laws enforced by them.

The women in Missouri and Indiana have just killed in committee eight-hour laws of this character; the women in Illinois are fighting to kill one, as are the women in Iowa, in Ohio they have scotched the minimum wage law by creating an investigatory commission; in New York we expect to do the same; in New Jersey the no-night-work law, despite the excellent work of the women to be affected by it, passed both houses, but was amended to “take effect December, 1924,”—a long interval in which we hope to bring forward such arguments, at the ballot box in November, 1923 and 1924, and in women’s clubs and the newspapers by educating women to the fact that the welfare of their sex will be seriously harmed by such laws.

Remember this: after years of “protective” legislation for women and minors the wages of women are at this time so far below those of men, for the same kind of work, that welfare seriously argue the necessity of a minimum wage law for women to raise the level of wages, although the highest award ever made by a Minimum Wage Board is $16.50! Instead of protecting the working woman, statutory discrimination tends to hamper her. The worst thing one can do is to place any handicap on the economic independence of a person. When women and children are tied together by legislation, their wage rates reflect that tie.

Giving women industrial equality will not disrupt home life; rather will it act as a check on the frequently over-arrogant male who, through “protective” parenthood and, through protective laws for women, access to a pay envelope, turns “home life” by his dictum.

If you don’t do as I say, I won’t give you the money for Johnny’s shoes—or the grocery bill!

Save us—and your daughters—from this domination of our most sacred instincts by making the path to earning a livelihood free from sentimental handicaps.

Women Are Fighting for Economic Equality

NEW JERSEY WOMEN WORKERS PRESENT STEVENS NO-NIGHT-WORK BILL

The wage-earning women of New Jersey appeared in force before the Senate hearing on the Stevens’ bill prohibiting night shift work to adult women, held at Trenton the afternoon of January 29, and told the committee that the proposed bill was in no sense a protection but was expressly designed to deprive them of work, although nearly all of the 200 women present were mothers, or had other dependents.

Those women who have a vital interest in the Stevens bill noted with amazement that the principal speaker for the bill, Mrs. Maud Swartz, president of the National Women’s Trade Union League (who was once a working woman but has for the past ten years been solely engaged in promulgating legislation designed to throw women out of work or to limit their earning power) collapsed in the middle of a speech purported to show that women working—for pay!—on night shifts must be unhealthy or immoral or both. After her recovery, Mrs. Swartz admitted that she had never worked on a night shift—thus proving on two counts that she did not know what she was talking about; but she did not admit that her organization was insincere in its stand against night work by telling the committee that the New York State Factory Investigating Personnel—“who have said that the car-papers to the contrary. Personnel managers, some of them, came from different plants in company with the women employees and spoke against the bill, one reading a letter of protest from the women in his plant who felt that they could not spend the money and time necessary to an appearance at Trenton.

Mrs. Mary A. Murray, president of the B. R. T. Women’s Equal Opportunity League, who has been a night worker for many years, spoke in favor of night shift work and against the Stevens bill; Amy Wren, a lawyer who has been counsel for the working women of New York and Brooklyn in their fights for amendments to labor laws of New York State for the past four years, in speaking against the bill tried to make the Federation of Labor’s spokesman admit that his arguments applied to men as well as to women, “regardless of sex,” being one of his oft-repeated sentence endings.

MADAME CARTEAUX BANNISTER  
THE FUTURE OF COL. SHAW IN BOSTON.

Buried with his negroes in the trench!—  
There he lies, a score of them around him;  
All the fires of bondage this shall quench,  
Could a monument so well have crowned him?

Sight to make a father's bosom throb—  
There he stands upon the canvass glowing!  
Sight to make a noble mother sob—  
Tender eyes, their glances on her throwing!

There he stands, so eloquent and mute,  
Modest, and yet looking in our faces,  
Unstirred and calmly, as doth suit  
One who did not ask the world's high places!

There he gazes, soldier-like and bold,  
There he stands, the man of color, bought and sold;  
Not a whit ashamed to die with him—  
Not a bit ashamed to lie with him!

Look upon him, Nation of the free!  
Surely this shall cure thee of thy meanness;  
Look upon him, Nation yet to be,  
Crying out remorseful, “Oh, my leanness!”

Sleep serenely, with thy country's sigh,  
Sleep serenely, to the nation given!  
Noble martyr! to the nation given,  
With thy little company on high;  
Thou shalt traverse all the plains of Heaven:

MADAME CARTEAUX BANNISTER.  
No. 31 Winter Street, Boston.

TO ABRAHAM LINCOLN.

“FREE LOVE,”

The Lord hath said it—Let my people go!

The earth and sky have answered with a shout  
Of rolling thunder—prayer—and trumpet song;  
Bannered pomp and nations on the march,  
Forever now repeating, Let them go!

Thou art the man! The Lord hath called thee up,  
Up from the people—out from all thy kind—  
And set thee on the outworks of a world,  
Where lighted thunderbolts and charging hosts,  
With garments rolled in blood, may try thy faith!

And now he saith to thee, unquailing chief,  
Thou chosen of the Lord, thou man of God!  
Anointed for this mighty work, stand fast!  
Lift up thine eyes. The vision is astir,  
Up to the hills, whence thy salvation cometh,  
And the embattled legions of the pit  
Come thundering on, O chief! and thou shalt see  
Chariots and horsemen, and the Lord of Hosts,  
The God of Jacob marshaling his power!

A. J. GROVER,

Our Government must say to the two hundred and fifty thousand braves of our African regiments, who, on the 1st of July, 1863, will wear the uniform, carry the arms and sustain the flag of a common country—

“There is a land for you, your children, and your children’s children.” And more, it must furnish free transportation to all who will go to Mexico, with the arms and sustain the flag of a common country.

The planting of our armed legions on those shores will meet with the approval of the world, and it will assure us that fair trial for all time.

The French Emperor will not stand by the house of Hapsburg, and could not do so successfully if he would. England cannot afford a hundred days’ war with this country, without having her world-wide commerce swept from the ocean, her towns in a state of riot, and her fairest provinces desolate. England, proud, unjust and domineering England!! She must pay, to the last dollar, the damages sustained by our commerce, in consequence of her permitting armed pirates to fit out in her ports.

We shall remember to demand payment of the losses sustained by our merchants, and, if she is withheld, England will see many of her first crafts follow the Alabama far down in the depths of the blue ocean, and such a war as will tax all her energies and resources for the next hundred years.

From France and England we shall find no interference with reference to Mexico. Long since would these powers have interfered, and sought to cause the permanent separation of our States, but from our marshalled hosts, our battle-fields, and the sublime energy of a nation, they have stood back with horror of consequences.

We have seen these European powers stand back with horror of consequences. The armies of the Union can defy a world in arms. How sure, then, is it, that beyond our present limits we need more room for the increasing millions of the South.

I allude not to Indiana and Illinois, with three millions of population; to Michigan, filled with life; to Wisconsin, and other Prairie States, now sending out a population. New Jersey, Delaware and Maryland will constantly increase the tide of emigration to other States.

The right to testify must be included in the foregoing enumeration as a part of the right to use the Courts, and several of the rights enumerated are certainly less vital and fundamental than the right to testify.

No one who has read the able opinion of Attorney General Bates, utterly abolishing the authority of the State to exclude colored men from the courts, can doubt that colored men may be citizens of the United States and of the several States; and, indeed, all the counsel in this case can seem to admit, that the petitioner is a citizen of Massachusetts.

This Court has no doubt that a citizen of Massachusetts has a right to demand the protection of his country, and that the use of the Courts of Virginia or any other State is manifestly essential to such protection.

The best blood of our country has already been spilled; our numbers are diminishing every day. The present campaign has cost us at least 80,000 men. How are these to be replaced? I see it stated in some of the papers, that if all those who owe service could be put in our armies, it would increase those by 200,000 men. But is this so? I doubt it, and I expect the majority are of my opinion. However, should the infiltration among the people of a number of free blacks, with the slaves of so large a number of plantations, and let their powers stand back with horror of consequences.

New England, New York, Pennsylvania and Ohio, at the end of forty years from this date, will contain fifty millions of people. Somewhere, that population must find room for its surplus men, women and children. Virginia, Kentucky, “The Old North State,” and Tennessee, with Mexico, will give to our race the wide-spread fields for fruits and flowers, and human happiness, afforded by the South.

The result of this war has satisfied the intelligent that the armies of the Union can defy a world in arms. How sure, then, is it, that beyond our present limits we need more room for the increasing millions of the South.

Let Mexico become the home of the Freedmen, and give to our race the wide-spread fields for fruits and flowers, and human happiness, afforded by the great table-land of Mexico, the rich and wide-spread States of the South.

Now comes the question: Will the negro fight?

Certainly he will. If the experience we have already had were not sufficient to prove it, let the experience we are now having prove it. The negro will fight, and fight well.

How will the army like the innovation? I have heard some of our men say they could not get along with negroes; but my opinion is, that our boys would get along with them better than with some of their fellow-citizens, and to place them upon the same footing with the Yankees, far down in the region of the “Halls of the Montezumas.”

United States District Court, District of Virginia,

LETTER TO A CALIFORNIA SENATOR.

San Francisco, (Cal.) Oct. 4, 1864.

Hon. George H. Williams:

Sir—In a recent address, delivered at Salem, Oregon, you predicted that the hour was near when the last man, woman and child would, before God, assert their freedom.

In company with the great mass of the American people, I thank you for feeling, thinking and expressing such sentiments. It is useless to hide our heads under the blanket. We are fighting for national independence, and not for slavery; and so I think, believes Mr. Davis—for the question of slavery, as an object of this war, has never, to my knowledge, appeared in any of his public documents.

Yours truly,  
LEICESTER A. SAWYER.

OPINION OF JUDGE UNDERWOOD,

ON THE RIGHT OF EXCLUDING THE TESTIMONY OF COLORED MEN FROM COURTS OF JUSTICE.

United States District Court, District of Virginia.

A person charged with an infamous crime could not be convicted by the testimony of any man unconvicted of an infamous crime.

The use of the Courts of the country and the right to give testimony in them are privileges so fundamental and important to the security of personal and domestic peace, as to make their denial one of the greatest wrongs which can be inflicted on a human being.

In the light of such guarantees, the enactment excluding the testimony of colored men from the courts of justice is utterly null and void, because it is entirely repugnant to her glorious Declaration of Rights, which, following the decision of Lord Mansfield, was adopted in June, 1776, as part of the Constitution of the State. Never has any question arisen, what would be the influence, or what tendency eventually to subvert our institution; or would it tend to preserve it? The institution of slavery is endangered by this war. There are twenty millions of people fighting for its extinction. There are a hundred millions applauding them! It is useless to hide our heads under the blanket. We are fighting for national independence, and not for slavery.

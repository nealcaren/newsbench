Tradition Dots the Whole Eastern Landscape With It, But Rhode
Island Has a Few That Are Well Authenticated

WOODROW Wilson has set 
the fashion in telling the
truth about Washington.
As a historian unswayed
by emotion and kindred
feelings, he has gone be-
yond the romance which has so com-
pletely enveloped the character of our
first President and drawn a picture of
the man that has been altogether too
realistic for those worshippers who like 
halos with their heroes in history.

As of the man, so of his movements
has a deal of untruth been uttered.
Rhode Island has contributed its share.
All over the State, as all up and down the
Atlantic coast, are houses in which
it is valiantly asserted Washington once
slept. Whereever one finds a house with
the date of erection preceding the
Revolution, one may confidently expect
to hear that at such and such a time
Washington was there and stayed through
the night.

But the facts do not make him out
such a continuous and persistent sleeper.
When the mists of tradition are brushed
away, the record shows that there were
just four places in the State, in which it
is certain that Washington spent some
time as a guest. Three were in Provi-
dence and one in East Greenwich. All
claims of the rest to fame are of
dubious foundation.

Although Washington stayed several
days on each of his visits to Newport,
there is nothing to indicate where he
rested o’ night. That he spread his
time as generously and as conveniently
as he was able among several of the
leading families there is little question.
But neither he nor contemporary writers
give any names; and to-day in Newport
only Trinity Church claims him as its
own.

Whether or not he went to church
while in the town by the sea is a prob-
lem that may never be solved satis-
factorily. The tradition of his visit to
Trinity and of the pew he occupied has
been paraded and then seemingly punc-
tured so many times that any arbitrary
attempt to settle it would revive a con-
troversy that is better quiescent. It
may be said in all fairness, however,
that the modern demand for historical
fact regardless of romance has had its
effect in the case of old Trinity and its
cherished memory of the past.

Washington liked Rhode Island and its
hospitality. He had many friends here.
Chief among them was Stephen Hopkins.

On reaching Newport he was met at

Members of New. District 1 Oraganiza-
tion Plan Canvas.

Members of the new Woman Suffrage
party of Providence in Ward 1, District
1, held their first regular meeting last
evening at the home of Mrs. Elizabeth
Monks, 159 Benefit street. A definite
plan was adopted for a regular canvass
of the district and the spreading of
woman suffrage propaganda.

At the opening of the meeting, which
was well attended, Miss Helen Thomas
was elected captain in the district, to
have direction of the details of the work
for the cause. A discussion of the meth-
ods used by suffrage workers in New
York, Chicago and other cities followed,
after which the members mapped out
plans for a similar campaign here.

The first thing that Washington did after the dying out of the excitement attendant upon his arrival was to visit the college grounds in company with Dr. Manning. In the evening he also went to the campus to view the illuminations. Next morning he showed his interest in the town’s principal industry by going down to one of the shipyards and viewing one of Brown & Ives’s big Indianmen then in the stocks. 
In the afternoon he and his party were guests at an entertainment and reception held in the Court House. Again 13 toasts were drunk and again the President remarked upon the hospitality of his friends in Providence. “It may be proper to remark,” added the Gazette, “that no untoward accident took place, that every countenance indicated the most heartfelt joy and that . we have reason to believe the President was perfectly satisfied with his reception.”

The following morning Washington set
sail in the Hancock for New York, never
again to honor either Providence or
Rhode Island by a visit.

There were two occasions when Wash-
ington touched Rhode Island soil that
he did not come to Providence. Both
times he was the guest of his old com-
rade in arms, Gen. James M. Varnum,
who built the Varnum mansion in East
Greenwich overlooking Cowesett bay.
Gen. Varnum was a jolly soul who loved
good things and who was noted for the 
punch that he brewed.

Once when Washington was on a visit
to Norwich, Conn., he made a detour in
order to greet Gen. Varnum and to sam-
ple some of the Varnum punch. At an-
other time when he was on his way
to Labanon, also in Connecticut, he
stopped over for a chat with the Gen-
eral. He slept in the state bedroom on
the second floor of the great house.
Here, also, Lafayette was a guest when
he came to Rhode Island.

There is one interesting feature in con-
nection with Washington’s visits to this
State. In none of them was any men-
tion ever made of his body-servant,
Cato, who enjoyed an annual death for
50 years, and more. There have been
nearly as many Catos as there are
houses where Washington slept. But no
native chronicler ever took the trouble
to speak of the original negro and
nobody, not even the most ardent pa-
trons of the Golden Ball Inn, ever as-
serted that Cato graced that hostelry
with his presence.

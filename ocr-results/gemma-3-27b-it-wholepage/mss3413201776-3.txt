**COLUMN 1**

Yet for all this, and all the noble virtues and radiance of personal character, which so universally distinguished that man, he could not, by any possibility, have been singled out by the American people as their chief magistrate. His private life, indeed, presented a succession of spotless examples of all that is virtuous and honorable; but it was perhaps not sufficiently known for such a lofty elevation. It would have been a mistake to have expected that his subtlety and refinement, his devotion to letters, his intense intellectual pursuits, were adapted to the rough and tumble of political strife, where strength, boldness, and popular appeal are generally the most effective weapons. The American people, though profoundly respecting character and intellect, had come to expect in their leaders, qualities of a different cast. They wanted a man who was capable of leading them by the force of his will, who could command attention by the magnetism of his presence, and who, at the same time, possessed principles and opinions commensurate with the hopes and expectations of the age.

Such a man the American people have found in James K. Polk. He possesses, in an eminent degree, the qualities of a practical and determined statesman; he is a man of strong convictions, and while he has the address to command respect, he has the boldness to assert his views and the the tenacity to maintain them. It is this combination of qualities that has secured him the confidence of his party and the suffrages of the nation.

We believe the election of Mr. Polk to be a call upon the nation to prosecute, with vigor and determination, the great work of national advancement, which has been so long delayed, and in this belief, we cordially unite with our fellow citizens in congratulating him upon the success which has attended his efforts.

**THE WHIG CONVENTION.**

The Massachusetts Whig Convention met at Worcester on Wednesday. Hon. Edward Everett was chosen chairman. Addresses were made by several prominent Whigs, advocating harmony and union in the party. Resolutions were adopted denouncing the administration and pledging support to Gen. Taylor.

**THE DEMOCRATIC CONVENTION.**

The Democratic State Convention met at Boston on Wednesday. Dr. Samuel Parkman was chosen President. A long and excited discussion took place on the subject of nominations. Finally, George N. Briggs was nominated for Governor, and Henry W. Lovering for Lieutenant Governor.

**COLUMN 2**

It has been for a long time the object of predatory ambition to reduce the United States to a condition of subserviency. Plans have been matured, and combinations formed, with a view to break up our Union, and to annex portions of our territory to foreign powers. In the midst of alarming conjectures, and when it was believed that the period for action had arrived, Mr. Polk was nominated for the Presidency. He held out the assurance that if elected, he would not only resist all attempts to encroach on our rights, but that he would, by all honorable means, endeavor to extend the territorial influence of the Union. The people responded to his call, and the result is, that we have a President who, we believe, is determined to vindicate the national honor, and to secure the blessings of liberty for generations to come.

His Inaugural Discourse is a document of remarkable clearness and force. It sets forth the principles upon which he intends to administer the government, and it breathes a spirit of determination that must command the respect of all who are not blinded by prejudice. 

He speaks, in terms of unyielding firmness, of the necessity of defending our rights on the Oregon territory; he expresses a desire to avoid unnecessary conflict with Mexico; but he declares that if Mexico persists in her refusal to treat with us, we shall not hesitate to adopt such measures as may be necessary to vindicate our claims.

He also expresses a strong desire to maintain peace with all nations; but he makes it perfectly clear that we will not submit to any insults or aggressions. 

**THE BRITISH STEAMER NIAGARA.**

The British Steamship Niagara, Captain Hobbs, arrived yesterday morning from Liverpool. She brings dates to the 8th inst.

The accounts from Ireland continue unfavorable. The famine still continues to rage, and the distress is appalling. Thousands of families are perishing from starvation and disease.

Her Majesty’s government is doing everything in its power to alleviate the suffering, but the extent of the distress is so great that all efforts are inadequate to meet the wants of the people.

The markets in England have been greatly depressed. Cotton has declined in price, and the demand for other articles has been very limited.

The political news is not of much importance. The Ministry have sustained a defeat in the House of Commons on an amendment to the address, but they still retain a working majority.

**FROM MEXICO.**

By the arrival of the steamship Yucatan, from Vera Cruz, we have dates to the 29th of October. 

The political situation in Mexico is extremely unsettled. The government is weak and divided, and the country is threatened with another revolution.

Santa Anna is still in power, but his authority is greatly diminished. He is accused of corruption and tyranny, and there is a general feeling of discontent throughout the country.

The Americans in Mexico are anxiously awaiting the result of the negotiations with the United States. They fear that if a war breaks out, they will be subjected to persecution and violence.

**THE MISSISSIPPI ELECTION.**

The returns from Mississippi show a decided victory for the Democratic party. The Democratic candidate for Governor, Albert G. Brown, is elected by a large majority.

**THE NEW-YORK POSTMASTER.**

The President has nominated Mr. John L. O’Sullivan, Editor of the Democratic Review, to be the Postmaster of New York.

But a briefful account of this important event, as it respects the political prospects of the nation, cannot fail to be instructive and useful to the community in which we live. The present struggle is between the advocates of progress and the champions of conservatism; between those who are disposed to embrace new ideas and those who are wedded to the old. In the midst of this conflict, Mr. Polk has been called upon to assume the reins of government. He is a man of strong ambition, and he is not likely to be deterred from pursuing his course by any obstacles that may lie in his way. 

He is a man of action, and he is not content with merely holding the office of President. He is determined to leave his mark upon the history of his country. And in this determination, he will receive the cordial support of the American people. 

The policy of the administration, as indicated in the Inaugural Discourse, is one of expansion and progress. Mr. Polk is a believer in the doctrine of Manifest Destiny, and he is determined to carry out that doctrine to its logical conclusion. 

He is also a strong advocate for a reduction of the duties on imports, and he is determined to effect a reform in the tariff.

The annual festival of the Boston Sunday School Union and the Children’s Aid Society was held yesterday afternoon at the Tremont Temple. A large attendance was present.

Rev. Mr. Parker, of the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions, opened the exercises with a prayer.

Hon. George L. Putnam, President of the Union, then read a report which gave a brief but interesting summary of the operations of the two societies during the past year. The report showed that the Union had distributed over 30,000 volumes of Sunday School literature, and that the Society had provided temporary homes and employment for over 200 children.

Speeches were made by several prominent gentlemen, including Hon. Samuel Kneeland, and Rev. Dr. Quincy.

The exercises were concluded with a hymn, sung by the children.

**COLUMN 4**

The election of Mr. Polk to the presidency, has been received with unqualified satisfaction by the Democratic party throughout the Union. It is hailed as a triumph of the principles which have always guided the Democracy; and it is believed that the new administration will be characterized by an honest, vigorous, and successful prosecution of the measures necessary to promote the prosperity and greatness of the nation. 

In his inaugural address, Mr. Polk has already given evidence of his determination to carry out the pledges which he made during the canvass. He has declared his intention to defend our rights on the Oregon territory; to settle our difficulties with Mexico; and to promote the interests of commerce and agriculture. 

He has also announced his intention to reduce the duties on imports, and to establish a more economical system of government. 

These are all measures which will tend to benefit the great body of the people. And we have no doubt that the new administration will receive the loyal support of all who desire to see our country prosperous and happy. 

**FROM CALIFORNIA.**

The steamer Crescent City has arrived at San Francisco from Canton, bringing dates to the 17th of August. 

The news from California is of the usual character. The gold mines are still producing large quantities of gold, and the population is rapidly increasing. 

The town of San Francisco is growing at an astonishing rate. New buildings are constantly being erected, and the streets are crowded with people. 

There is a great demand for provisions and other supplies, and prices are very high.

**THE LATE FIRE IN NEW YORK.**

A destructive fire occurred in New York on Wednesday night, which consumed several buildings on Pearl street. 

The loss is estimated at $200,000.

**THE SLAVE TRADE.**

The British government is taking active measures to suppress the slave trade. Several vessels engaged in the slave trade have recently been captured by British cruisers.

THE WEEKLY REGISTER.

VOL. XXIX.—NO. 33.] PHILADELPHIA, SATURDAY, AUGUST 12, 1865. [WHOLE NO. 817.

The Condition of the Southern Constiution—Requalified Voters—How the Ku-Klux Act will operate.—It is understood at Washington that the preliminary steps towards the reconstruction of the Southern States have been substantially agreed upon and will soon be put into practical operation. The basis is this:

First—That the insurgents shall lay down their arms and fully submit to the authority of the United States.

Second—That they shall formally abolish slavery and ratify the constitutional amendment prohibiting it forever in the United States.

Third—That they shall ratify the amendment defining citizenship and protecting the rights of citizens in all the States.

Fourth—That each State shall remodel its constitution and laws so as to be republican in form–that is, to guarantee the right of suffrage to all persons of color.

Fifth—That a certain number of citizens—say one-fifth—shall take an oath of allegiance to the United States.

The plan, as we understand, does not require a unanimous vote in any State, but only a sufficient vote to show that a majority of its people are sincerely repentant of their rebellion. No attempt, it is said, will be made to punish individuals for their participation in the late rebellion, except where there is evidence of personal guilt of flagrant crimes.

REMOVAL OF THE PUBLIC PRINTING.

The contract for printing the public documents for the next three years has been awarded to Messrs. R. & J.E. Taylor. The amount is $838,855.

The Washington correspondent of the Pittsburg Gazette says: “Mr. Stanton has had a bit of a fight with the President over the removal of Mr. Defrees as Public Printer. Stanton wanted Defrees to remain, but the President decided that his removal was necessary to make room for a more efficient man.”

THE PALATINE PILGRIMS.

A Paris letter writes that the Palatine pilgrims, who were the ancestors of the American, mostly New Yorkers, were driven from their homes by Louis XIV. in the 17th century, and fled for refuge to Holland and England. From thence they were invited by William Penn to settle in the state of New York, who gave them lands, and allowed them to worship God according to the dictates of their conscience.

The Correspondence of the Boston Transcript Gives an interesting account of the Bounty jumpers from Massachusetts: “It has surprised many to learn the enormity of the system of desertion and bounty jumping that carried on. The total amount of bounty paid to Massachusetts volunteers is stated to be $12,545,589. Of this amount $4,249,958 68 was paid as a state bounty, and the remainder as a United States bounty. It is estimated that at least $2,000,000 have been secured by imposter and deserters. The number of re-enlistments, largely acquired by this means, went to swell the ranks of the army at the close of the war, and no one probably suspected the actual state of affairs. It is said that the Provost Marshals have a list of 15,000 names of those who desert and re-enlist under assumed names.”

A dispatch from Nashville, Tenn., states that the wholesale arrests of former rebel officers under the orders of Gen. Sherman has been suspended.

LETTER FROM PRESIDENT LINCOLN.

HAGERSTOWN, MD., July 18, 1865.

My Dear Mr. Collins:

I had the pleasure to receive to-day a visit from your brother, and was truly glad to meet him. It is a great pleasure to meet the sons and daughters of those who have labored so devotedly in laboring men. I have been impressed with the mission of the American Freedmen's Union Commission, and the evidence which my friend, Jacob R. Shipherd, Esq. gave me of its character and operations, satisfied me of its immeasurable value. It is an astonishing thing to think that among us here in America, our brethren are subjected to such hardship and deprivation. I have always regarded the welfare of the colored race as among the most important objects of our national care and attention. It is our duty to give them the benefit of education, and all the rights of citizenship. It is not, however, sufficient to merely grant them these rights, but we must also provide them with the means of enjoying them. The Freedmen's Union Commission is doing a great work in this direction, and I heartily commend its efforts to the generous support of the American people.

Yours truly,

A. LINCOLN.

To Rev. James L. Collins, Boston.

CHANGE IN THE NAVAL REGULATIONS.

Washington, July 29.—The Navy Department has issued an order making the time of enlistment in the Navy five years, including a year’s cruise. After three years’ service, a sailor will be entitled to thirty days’ furlough. Should he return to duty, he will be credited with the additional two years’ service. Whenever a large supply of seamen is needed, the Captains of vessels will be authorized to enlist men for two years, at a comparatively higher rate of pay.

ELECTION IN PENNSYLVANIA, INDIANA AND OHIO.

The elections in Pennsylvania, Indiana and Ohio are attracting much attention, and are regarded as testing the sentiment of the public towards the Administration. The Republicans have lost largely in Pennsylvania. The legislature refuses to ratify the constitutional amendment. The Democratic majority in the Senate is 39, and in the House 116.

Indiana has also gone heavily Democratic–the whole Republican ticket is defeated by large majorities. The Democrats have gained the legislature. 

Ohio remains steadfastly Republican. The election resulted in a victory for the Republicans by about 30,000 majority.

REBELS IN MEXICO.

Reports from Mexico indicate that a considerable number of rebels are still flocking to that country, where they are plotting schemes against the United States. It is said that the Emperor Maximilian is secretly encouraging these rebels, and furnishing them with arms and ammunition.

A desperate attempt to rob the Pacific Express train was made on the night of the 22nd ult., between Placerville and Sacramento. The robbers were repulsed with a loss of two of their number killed.

MR. SUMNER’S SPEECH.

At a dinner given in Boston on the 26th ult., by the Massachusetts Union League Club, Senator Sumner delivered an eloquent speech, in which he traversed the history of slavery, and urged that the safety of the Union depended on the complete and permanent emancipation of the slaves. He said; “We have achieved a great victory in the abolition of slavery, but it is not yet a permanent victory. The slaveholders are still in the South, and they are determined to restore their old power. We must not allow them to do so. We must ensure that the slaves are completely and permanently emancipated. We must also see to it that they are protected in their rights as citizens.”

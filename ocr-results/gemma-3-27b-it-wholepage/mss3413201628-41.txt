THE Pine Tree.

By JOHN G. Whittier.

Life again! The woods are bowing
Low before the morning’s glowing,
And the earth her tribute brings
To the sun, on hopeful wings.

Eastward, through the silver haze,
Echoes from the olden days—
George and Bartholomew,
Keep their watches now.

Over Hampton’s windswept shore, 
Answered England’s royal shore—
“Hush!—the signal clove the spray—
Freedom’s hymn—a glorious day!”

Spirit of the Pine Tree—free—
Telling tales of liberty!
How the sturdy, steadfast heart
Yields to storm but doth not part.

So let us, like that emblem, grow,
Our roots in truth—our heads in glow—
Ever whispering to the past—
Freedom’s tree—forever cast!

“A late ecclesiastic conference,” says the ‘Connecticut

solutions, the views which we would be glad to see a
general adoption of. They are to the effect, that the

uncalled for; and unanimously pledged themselves to
labor for the repression of the anti-Slavery movement
within their bounds.”

It is not to be concealed, that in certain quarters,
there seems to be a rising aversion to the plain speaking
and persevering advocacy of abolition principles.
Some seem to think that the cause of abolition is un-
favorable to the progress of the Gospel, and is likely to
create divisions in the church. They dare not, how-
ever, utter these sentiments openly; but they betray them
by their efforts to silence and censure those who are
bold and faithful in speaking the truth as it is in Jesus.

They wish to have a *present* heaven without a *future*
Heaven. 

**THE DEBATE—Continued**

From the New York Evangelist.

THE WAR BETWEEN ABOLITIONISTS AND THEIR
OPPONENTS.

“The Constitution and Slavery.”

We propose to continue the discussion of this impor-
tant subject, and shall address ourselves, in the first
instance, to the position assumed by the Abolitionists,
that the Constitution *forbids* all legislation on the sub-
ject of slavery. The Abolitionists, as we understand,
affirm, that the Constitution does not authorize the
existence of slavery, and that any law recognizing its
existence, is, *ipso facto*, null and void.

We beg to submit to our readers, that this is a position
that does not rest upon a very solid foundation. It is
evident, from the tenor of the instrument, that slavery
existed at the time of its adoption. The same states,
that were permitted to count their slaves in determining
their representation in Congress, were also allowed to
import them until the year 1808.

We will hazard nothing as to the motives, that influenced
the framers of the Constitution. We maintain, how-
ever, that the Constitution *recognizes* the existence of
slavery in those states where it already existed. This
recognition, it will be said, was a concession to the
Slaveholders; but a concession, which was deemed
necessary to prevent a dissolution of the Union. 

It is a matter of well known fact, that the states had
reserved to themselves the right to govern themselves,
in all matters which did not violate the Constitution of
the United States. Slavery was, in reality, a domestic
institution; and the Constitution did not attempt to
interfere with the domestic institutions of the states. 

We are aware, that many object to the position that
the framers *intended* to recognize slavery. But we
submit to our readers, whether it is not a more natural
and reasonable interpretation of the Constitution, to
suppose that it was intended to recognize slavery, than
to assume that the framers intended to prohibit it.

**FOR THE LIBERATOR.**

An Appeal to the Christians of the United States. 

by Rev. Joshua Leavitt.

Fellow-Christians:—I come to you with no ro-
mantic representations of the sufferings of the slave, 
nor with a vain attempt to rouse you by exaggerated
depictions of its horrors. I content myself with pre-
senting a plain, unvarnished statement of its real hardship, 
as it exists under the sanction of your own laws, and of 
your own national institutions.

I ask you to look at the slave, not as a chattel, or as an
inferior being, but as a fellow-man—a brother—a mem-
ber of the same human family. I ask you to recognize 
his rights, not as a legal privilege to be bartered away, 
but as God-given endowments, that no earthly power 
has a right to deprive him of.

I ask you to consider the injunction of our Lord—
“Whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do 
ye even so to them.” Would you willingly be the subject 
of another's arbitrary will? Would you endure the lash 
of the driver? Would you permit your children to be 
sold from your arms? And yet, these and far other 
horrors are the daily realities of the slave’s existence.

I ask you to remember the great principles of the 
Gospel—principles of universal benevolence, of equal 
rights, and of impartial justice. These principles are 
being violated by the continued existence of slavery. 

If you boast the blessings of the Gospel, and the 
privileges of a free Government, while you continue to 
hold your fellow-men in bondage, you are guilty of a 
gross inconsistency, and a terrible hypocrisy. 

Let the cause of liberty be advanced, not by the 
bayonet of the soldier, nor by the fire of the demagogue 
but in an enlightened and Christian manner. 

**MR. BIRNEY’S LECTURE.**

Reports from various sections of the country give 
us the most flattering accounts of Mr. Birney’s Lect-
ures. At Haverhill, Massachusetts, it is said that 
his discourses were highly attended, and excited an 
interest equal to the most enthusiastic revival meeting. 
At Lexington, in the same state, the Court-house 
was crowded. This is but a specimen. Mr. B. will 
surely awaken the conscience of the North.

---

If you look into the workings of slavery, the failings of 
men, the delusive promises of the Bible, and the fear of 
contradicting popular sentiment, you will discover why 
some Christians hesitate to act out the impulse of their 
hearts. But the Bible is plain, and the claims of justice
are imperative. It is high time that all the faithful  
followers of the Lord should take a stand.

The popular delusion, that slavery is a political 
question, and that it is not the business of Christians 
to meddle with it, is one of the most insidious and 
dangerous errors of the age. It is a moral question—a 
question of human rights—a question of eternal justice—
and it concerns every Christian in the world.

The true method of abolishing slavery is to enlist the 
power of Christianity. By awakening the consciences of 
slaveholders and slave-buyers, by changing the public 
sentiment, and by creating a general determination to 
extirpate the evil, we may accomplish this great work. 

Let us, therefore, unite our efforts, and, with a firm 
and unwavering resolve, put forth our strength to 
abolish slavery, and to bring this nation into harmony 
with the principles of the Gospel. 

Joshua Leavitt.

December 10, 1838

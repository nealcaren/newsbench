**Poetry.**

THE NATION'S JUDGMENT.

"Oh, Father! must we always see
A dark and bitter destiny?
Must wrong forever triumph still,
And Justice bow to power and will?
Must innocence, with tearful eye,
Be crushed beneath the oppressor's cry?
Must virtue weep, exhausted, lone,
And vice exult upon her throne?
Must peace be ever bought with blood,
And brotherhood misunderstood?"

A whispered voice replies, "Behold,
I give to thee a glimpse of gold!
Not in the ruling of the strong,
But in the righting of the wrong.
Not in the pomp and pride of state,
But where the humble congregate!
Not in the triumph of the few,
But in the hopes of me and you.
The Nation's judgment, long delayed,
Is swift to come, but justly made."

“And what,” we ask, “those instruments 
Of torture, wrath, and vile intents?
Those laws that bind the weak and poor,
And give the rich a wider door?
Those customs, born of selfish need,
That plant the tares of bitter seed?"

The voice responds, "These are the tools
That rise against the basic rules.
When blinded Justice leads the way,
And dark ambition holds its sway,
Then surely will a time be found
When these shall fall, and truth abound!"

**The Liberator.**

RATIONALISM IN THE PULPIT. NO. I.

MR. EDITOR—Deism, Rationalism, and what is called the anti-supernatural in religion, are subjects of much discussion in these times. The ill-conceived idea frequently exists that they are novel doctrines, propagated by a class of thoughtless rebels against the established order. Such is not the fact. These are the oldest forms of religious thought. They were the religious system of the most refined and for centuries have had advocates in every denomination, and are to-day most respectably represented in the most intelligent and virtuous of our population. It is not, however, our purpose to write an elaborate history of these doctrines, but rather to present a statement of what they are, and why they are held by those who embrace them.

We proceed with the supposition that the universe is governed by fixed laws, established by its Creator, and that these laws are uniform in their operation. We assume that there is no such thing as a “miracle”—that is, a suspension or violation of these laws. We believe that all that is necessary to be known relating to God and our duty, may be discovered by the exercise of reason and the study of his works. We cannot conceive of a God who would perform an act wholly inconsistent with his own establishment, or by which he would give encouragement to unfounded belief. 

It is said that the doctrine of a personal God is incompatible with rationalism. The most we contend for is that we cannot know the manner of his existence. We assume that he exists, and that he is the author of what we observe in nature. We believe that the contemplation of his works is the most sublime and profitable occupation of the human mind; and that the life which is in harmony with moral principles, is the most acceptable offering of devotion. 

We do not deny the existence of inspiration, but we understand it to be simply the highest degree of intellectual excitement, similar to the fervor and zeal which sometimes attends the pursuit of literary or political objects. In the Bible we recognize a collection of the writings of good and wise men, who were imbued with a passion for truth and righteousness. We consider it a work of great value and influence, but we do not believe it to be the sole and infallible rule of faith. 

We are not disposed to depreciate the importance of Christian charity, or to be intolerant of the opinions of others. We simply claim the right to think for ourselves, and to express our opinions without fear of persecution or reproach. We have no desire to disturb the faith of others, but we will not consent to wear the chains of prejudice, or to renounce the claims of our own intellect. 

Yours, with respect,

LEICESTER A. SAWNER.

NEWS FROM PENNSYLVANIA.

A correspondent, writing from Philadelphia, under date of November 2d, states that the annual meeting of the Pennsylvania Anti-Slavery Society was crowded with members, and that a sense of deep feeling prevailed throughout the proceedings. The report of the Executive Committee was read, and resolutions were adopted, expressing a determined adherence to the principles of immediate abolition.

A large and enthusiastic meeting was subsequently held in Arch Street Meeting House, where speeches were delivered by several prominent abolitionists. The proceedings were productive of a highly encouraging effect, and the cause of emancipation received a valuable impulse.

NEW ENGLAND ANTI-SLAVERY SOCIETY.

A meeting of the Executive Committee of the New England Anti-Slavery Society was held at the office of the Society, in Boston, on Wednesday, Nov. 3d. Present – Garrison, chairman; Collins, George Bradburn, and others.

The minutes of the last meeting were read and approved. A report from the agent, relative to the collection of funds, was presented, and it was determined to make further efforts to obtain the necessary means for carrying on the operations of the Society. It was further resolved to publish an address to the people of New England, urging them to take a more active and energetic interest in the cause of abolition.

We have witnessed some circumstances illustrative of this thought. A slaveholder, whose relations to this institution we shall not disclose, having, some years ago, suddenly felt himself morally convincted of its sin, was violently opposed by his family. They threatened to disown him, to drive him from his home, and to ruin his temporal interest. Under their combined pressure, he yielded to their will, abandoned his principles, and returned to the practice of oppression. But that very year, a blight fell on his crops, disabling him from making a meagre harvest. His children, one after another, were stricken down by disease, and one infant died in his arms. He then said—"I have brought these afflictions upon myself by receding from my duty."

We also keep in view what David says, in the 25th Psalm: “Unto thee lift I up my eyes, O thou that makest heaven and earth.” But he adds, “Guide me in thy truth, and teach me thy way.” There is a tendency in some to overrate the faculty of self-guidance. But we see a lesson in the story of the Apostle Paul. An extraordinary light broke upon him while on his way to Damascus; thus making him blind, and lead, by the hand, to that city, where he was enlightened and restored to sight. 

In contemplation of the cause of abolition, we would ask all our readers to adore the unsearchable depth of the Divine Wisdom, and to place an entire and ever-lasting confidence in his redeeming love.

**PHILANTHROPY.**

The following letter was laid upon our table this morning:

“NEW HOPE, Oct. 26th, 1835.
“Dear Friend Garrison:

I send you five dollars to aid in the glorious work of emancipation. I am a poor, humble, labouring man, and cannot do much, but I am earnestly wishing for the speedy downfall of slavery. My heart is with you and with all who are engaged in this righteous cause.

This money I earned by extra industry, that I might have something to dedicate to the Lord and to the cause of freedom. May God bless you and yours, and hasten the day when every slave shall breathe the air of liberty.

Yours, in the spirit of abolition,

ELIJAH V. STONE.”

We think as yet their spirit is tame. It is tame in comparison to the magnitude of the wrong; and it is so naturally. To cast off the foundations of society and to change the habits of years require courage and deliberation. It would not be unnatural if all were shaken. 

A letter from South Carolina states that the planters from that State have now begun seriously calculating on a decline in the price of cotton. They are apprehensive that the agitation in the North, and the increased attention paid to the investigation of the claims of the slaveholders, will have the effect of diminishing the demand for their staple commodity. They also fear a general insurrection of the slaves, should the abolitionists continue to agitate the question of emancipation.

THE ANTI-SLAVERY STRUGGLE IN SOUTH CAROLINA.

A correspondent, writing from Charleston on the 8th inst., transmits the following particulars:

“You will perceive by the proceedings of a public meeting lately held here, that the people of this city are beginning to reflect on the subject of slavery. The opposition to the abolitionists is still strong, but it is not so violent as it was at first. Some of the leading citizens have begun to express their doubts as to the wisdom of continuing the institution of slavery. 

There is an increasing anxiety among the slaveholders, especially the capitalists. They see that the day is approaching when slavery will be abolished, and they are making preparations to provide for the coming crisis. They are investing their money in Northern manufactures, and they are sending their slaves to other States, whenever they can find a purchaser. 

The abolitionists, on the other hand, are growing bolder and more determined. They are distributing anti-slavery pamphlets and tracts, and they are holding public meetings to discuss the subject of emancipation. They are also making efforts to enlist the sympathies of the white population in their cause.

The contest, as you will readily perceive, is a fierce one. But the abolitionists are confident that they will ultimately prevail. They believe that the moral sentiments of the age are opposed to slavery, and they are determined to continue the struggle until the institution is abolished.”

We have received a pamphlet, entitled “A Narrative of the Sufferings of David Walker.” Edited by his son, David Walker, Jr. This is an extraordinary publication and will be particularly interesting to those who desire to learn something of the spirit and character of the colored population of this country.

**Temperance.**

Our space compels me to be very brief this week, but I cannot omit to say, that the cause of temperance is onward. A meeting was held in Faneuil Hall last week, which was attended by a large and respectable audience. Many pledged themselves to abstain from all intoxicating drinks. This is a very good beginning, but much more must be done before we can hope to rid our land of this dreadful curse.

A. C.

FIFTH SECTION. THE PROVIDENCE SUNDAY JOURNAL, FEBRUARY 16, 1919.

“THE HOUSE WHERE WASHINGTON SLEPT”

Tradition Dots the Whole Eastern Landscape With It, But Rhode Island Has a Few That Are Well Authenticated

ITHIN a radius of a few miles of Providence there are half a dozen houses claimed to have sheltered Washington in his tours of New England.  It is also said that he ate a chicken dinner at the Old Kent County Court House, and that his favorite dish consisted of boiled potatoes. Such tales are part of the local color, and they cannot be dismissed as pure invention.  Washington did make trips through New England during the Revolution and immediately after. He visited Rhode Island twice – once in 1776 and again in 1781. He was always escorted by a suite and was the guest of either the state authorities or of some prominent citizen.

Of course, the host will be remembered, and the house in which he slept will acquire a certain degree of historical importance. But the process of attaching a romantic tradition to a commonplace occurrence is very easy. It is not difficult to imagine a landlord telling his guests that General Washington once graced that very room. It is even possible that Washington himself encouraged the idea, for a modest man is not always averse to having a few pleasant stories told about himself.

The distinction between a well-established fact and a charming legend is often blurred with the passage of time.  In the case of the houses said to have sheltered Washington, the evidence varies greatly. Some rest on solid documentary proof, while others are based on nothing more than local lore.  

But there is one house in East Greenwich which, despite the scepticism of some historians, stands on a firmer basis than most. This is the Gibson House, a typical colonial mansion which has been carefully preserved, and which is to-day one of the most interesting landmarks in Rhode Island.

The story of Washington’s visit to the Gibson House on December 5, 1781, is supported by a number of contemporary documents.  The late William R. Staples, in his “History of East Greenwich,” states that Washington “lodged one night in the house” presently owned by the heirs of John Gibson.  The diary of Judge William Ellery, a well-known figure in Rhode Island history, contains an entry dated December 5th, 1781, stating that Washington “took up his quarters” at the house of John Gibson.  

Furthermore, there is a bill in the Rhode Island Historical Society, made out by John Gibson to General Washington, for the sum of £12 10s, for “entertainment.”  The bill lists the items charged for -- “1 supper, 1 breakfast, straw for horse, and lines.” To the items for man ad beast were added also “one bottle of rum” and “two bottles of wine,” seemingly confirming the tradition of Washington’s tastes. 

The Gibson House was built in 1738 by John Gibson, who was a merchant and a prominent citizen of East Greenwich. The house is a fine example of colonial architecture, with its symmetrical façade, its central chimney, and its spacious rooms. It is built of wood, and is painted white. The windows are small, with multi-paned sashes. The front door is surrounded by an elaborate carved frame. 

The house has been carefully preserved by the Gibson family, and it is now open to the public as a museum. The furniture is largely original, dating back to the colonial period. The rooms are furnished in the style of the 18th century. The house contains a collection of portraits, furniture, and other artifacts relating to the history of East Greenwich.

“Farm House in East Greenwich to Which Washington Paid a Visit”

“Washington’s visit was a brief one, but it was not without incident.  According to local tradition, a young girl named Betsy, who was a servant in the house, was so overcome with awe at the sight of the great general that she fainted.  Washington, upon seeing her fall, rushed to her assistance and revived her with a cordial.

The story of Betsy’s fainting spell is probably apocryphal, but it adds to the charm of the story.  And even if there is no documentary evidence to support it, it is not entirely unbelievable.  Washington was a commanding figure, and it is not surprising that he should have inspired a certain amount of reverence in those who met him.

The Gibson House is not the only house in Rhode Island that claims to have sheltered Washington.  There are others in Newport, Bristol, and Providence.  But the Gibson House is unique in that it has a substantial amount of documentary evidence to support its claim.  

The fact that Judge Ellery, a contemporary of Washington, recorded the visit in his diary, and that John Gibson sent Washington a bill for his entertainment, are enough to establish the fact beyond any reasonable doubt.

The Gibson House is a reminder of a bygone era, and it is a valuable piece of Rhode Island history.  It is a house that should be preserved for the enjoyment of future generations.

WAR D 1 APPROPRIATE'S MOVE
Owners of Property on Dartmouth
and Thayer Street Give Notice
of Intention to Secure Rights.
Acting on Request of Residents.

The committee from the property
owners on Dartmouth and Thayer
streets Monday afternoon completed
the preliminary organization for the
suit to secure a mathematical ruling
on the pending proposition to widen
Dartmouth street and Thayer street.
From most of the owners on the
two streets a willingness has been
shown to cooperate in the matter.
The plan is to secure an injunc-
tion against the city in this case to
prevent the taking of the land, an
order that has not been made.
The action is taken as a pro-
tective measure. Guardian W. A.
Richmond of the law firm of Card
and Richmond is the attorney for
the owners. Because of the court
holiday the date of the hearing has
not yet been arranged.

The strain that the roads were placed
under during the recent prolonged
storm has brought about measures
on the part of the city, and at a
meeting of the mayor and board of
aldermen held Monday, large appro-
priations were made to relieve the
conditions in various parts of the
city.  About $20,000 has been set
aside for this work. 

Six teams were put at work along
Branford street last night and to-day.
This section of the city has been
given some attention for several
days.  The work is being carried on
by the Department of Public Works
and requires the employment of
several men.  Materials also are
needed for the work, and there is
a large order out for gravel. 

The streets of Providence are not in
nearly as bad condition as they were
a week ago, and the situation is fast
improving.  With the warmer weather
that has prevailed since Sunday, the
work of clearing the snow has been
easier.  

Other streets in the city that are
particularly in need of attention
include Chalkstone avenue and
Brook street.  Arrangements are
being made to attend to these roads
in the next few days.

But No More Than One-Room Schoolhouse Were Washington's Days Able to Afford.

Now Shown at the House, One of a Notable Collection of Early School Things

“The collection of displaying articles,
representing the method of teaching
in the early one-room schoolhouses
of Rhode Island, is on exhibition
at the Gibson House. The collection
was made by Miss Ella M. Good-
win, formerly a teacher in the
schools of East Greenwich. It
includes school seats, desks, books,
charts, slates and other articles
used in the schoolrooms of that period. 

The collection is of interest, not
only to those who attended the
one-room schools, but to all who are
interested in the history of education
in Rhode Island.

The days of the one-room school-
house are long past, but they were
days of hard work and sacrifice.
The teachers were poorly paid and
the schools were poorly equipped.
But the children of those days received
a good education and they were
taught the value of hard work and
self-reliance. 

The Gibson House is open to the
public on Tuesdays and Saturdays
from 2 to 5 o’clock.  Visitors are
welcome.

**COLUMN 1**

A song, my friends, a song indeed—
A song for all the world to heed!
A song in hearts of purest gold—
A song that, when rightly told,
Will make the deaf ear hear,
And open wide the eyes of the blind!
A song for the wretched, and the week,
And the shattered heart, and the broken cheek!
A song for many sorrows, sung,
By many a trembling, tearful tongue!
A song for the lonely, the poor.
And all who can never more
Hope for a brighter, happier shore—
A song of cheer, for evermore!

THE PICTURE OF COL. SHAW IN BOSTON.
The unveiling of the statuary of Col. Robert G. Shaw, at Boston, on the 14th inst., was a memorable event.  The ranks of the public were filled with prominent citizens, and the procession was very large.  The ceremony was made impressive and touching by the presence of the veteran colored soldiers who were under Col. Shaw's command.  There were tears and gladness, and congratulations, and a feeling of appreciation of the valor of those who fought, and of the sacrifice of those who fell, in the struggle for freedom. The statue is said to be a fine work of art, and it is rightly located—in the spot where the brave Colonel led his men, and in the city that was ever true to freedom.

He was a friend of temperance, and a supporter of all benevolent movements.  His hopes were always fixed upon a brighter future for the African race.  He lived, and he died, a Christian—believing in the efficacy of the atonement, and trusting in the merit of the Savior.  His last days were marked by a rare serenity and peace.  He knew that he was dying, but he had no fear; no regret; and no pain, except as it was incidental to the disease that was slowly, but surely, undermining his physical powers.  He spoke of death as a desirable release—as a welcome entrance into the realms of everlasting life.  And thus he passed away—a good man—a true philanthropist—a faithful servant of God.

PROMOTION OF FREEDOM
The citizens of Boston, recognizing the importance of organizing the colored people of the South into a constituent and reliable force in the reconstruction of those States, held a meeting on Thursday evening, at the Tremont Temple, and formed “The New England Society for the Propagation of Freedom among the Colored People.” The resolutions passed were all well-considered and are of such a helpful nature that we copy them for the inspection of our readers.
Resolved, That we recognize the obligations of the nation to provide for the permanent security of the rights and liberties of the freed people of the South.
Resolved, That we sympathize with the efforts of the American Missionary Association to promote education and civilization among the freed people, and pledge to them our continued co-operation and support.
Resolved, That we deem it of the highest importance to encourage the self-reliance and industrial independence of the freed people.
Resolved, That we consider the extension of suffrage to the colored men of the South an act of justice and expediency, and urge its prompt and universal adoption.

**COLUMN 3**

A correspondent writes from Athens, Ga., and says: “The excitement here is abating, but the people are not yet reconciled to the reconstruction of the State. The Union League is very active, and is endeavoring to organize the laborers. General Terry has given his approval to the constitution ratified today. It does not confer the right of suffrage upon the colored people, but takes it away from those who participated in the rebellion.”

THE REBEL RAIDERS.
A dispatch from Alton, Ills., of the 14th inst., states that a large band of rebel raiders have been operating along the Illinois Central railroad, between Jackson and Carbondale. They have robbed several trains, and have also committed other depredations.  A large posse of citizens has been organized to pursue the raiders, and it is hoped they will soon be captured.

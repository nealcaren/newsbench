Ella M. Sherwin Speaks Before
League of Women Voters
Monday, March 12, the League of
Women Voters of New York City held
its annual convention at Hotel Penn-
sylvania. Mrs. Ada R. Wolff had been
asked to speak on Industrial Equality
principles, but was unable to do so be-
cause of a previous engagement, and
Miss Ella M. Sherwin, corresponding
secretary of the Women’s Equal Op-
portunity League, took up the verbal
cudgels in defense of our firm stand
against so-called protective legislation.
Miss Sherwin is a member of the
League of Women Voters of Brooklyn
and her uncompromising attitude
against 8-hour and minimum wage
bills for women is concurred in by
many other women who are members
of the Women Voters League, in this
citv and elsewhere.
Miss Sherwin’s speech was listened
to with much interest by her audience
and after the meeting many womer
told her thev believed she was right.
Mrs. Frances Perkins, Industrial
Commissioner, spoke in the affirma-
tive, but, as at the hearing in Albany
recently, all her arguments and pleas
were for the “flapper” in industry —
and we have always contended that the
he-flapper and the she-flapper should
be prevented, through restrictive legis-
lation, from leaving school too soon
and from entering industry in com-
petition with adults. A forty-eight-
hour law for minors of both sexes up
to 18 would be a proper exercise of the
State’s authority over its wards, and a
minimum wage law for such minors
would discourage their employment.
The League passed a resolution en-
dorsing the eight-hour and minimum
wage bills now pending in New York
State Legislature — said resolution
being passed BEFORE Miss Sherwir
spoke. Verbum satis sapienti!

Speech of Irene Paquet Before the
Providence League of Women Voters.
March 20. 1923
What the wage-earning woman needs
is full equality with wage-earning men
—and as any woman is liable to be
“thrown on her own” at any time, by
an act of Nature or the cussedness of
human nature, wage-earning women
means any woman, from the highest
to the lowest in social position, from
the richest to the poorest in this world’s
goods.
Those who advocate special legisla-
tion for women in industry are, strange-
ly enough, quite certain that women
need and must have political equality
in civil law. While claiming that
woman is able to exercise the fullest
control over her children, her home,
her business. if she owns it herself: her
Industrial Equality
bankbook, her vote, her hours spent in
political spellbinding, these illogicians
say that as soon as she goes into the
factory to make—for pay—what her
grandmother was obliged to make, for
only her board and keep, in the past;
as soon as she goes into the store to
sell—for pay—what her grandmother
sold in barter to itinerant peddlers, then
woman suffers a change in her status
and becomes a subject for laws and
regulations that make her of no more
value to her employer than a_ child
under sixteen and forever preclude her
from receiving equal pay since she is
forbidden by law to perform ‘equal
work.
After an intensive investigation ex-
tending over many months the National
Woman's Party has arrived at this
unanimous conclusion: All the laws pur-
porting to be in favor of women are
in PRACTICE, if not ‘n theory, ONE
OF THE WEAPONS TO EXPLOIT
WOMEN,
In the District of Columbia, which
has an eight-hour law (this does not
forbid night-shift work, however) and
a minimum wage law for women and
minors, women have been replaced by
men in many occupations, because
employers can work the men for
longer hours at overtime rates, while
the woman must stop on the hour
or the employer will be fined. So men,
and boys over 16, get the jobs; and this
is the case in other states having such
laws, and will be the case in your state
if the Lavender eight-hour bill for min-
ars and women hecomes law.
An eight-hour day by arrangement
between the employer and his em-
ployees, such as many of your mills
and stores now have, and which ap-
plies to all the employees without sex
distinction, is the only fair kind of hour
regulation, and if the labor leaders
were on the level that is the only kind
they would desire; but those of you
who attended the hearings at the State
House and listened to these men plead
fora LAW putting women on an earn-
ing basis with children, at the same
time bitterly attacking the “persons”
bill aiming to give men and women
the same desirable working hours, can
have no illusions as to what these mer
really want: the industrial subjection
of women; high wages for themselves
based on the lowered wages of the
women, and all the overtime, at time
and a half rates, denied to the womer
hv law.
Arouse yourselves to the danger of
such laws—women must no longer be
the regulated half of humanity. Be-
cause we are mothers, actual and po-
tential, we must be free and able to
earn as much as man, when necessary,
in order to give our children the proper
food, education and environment. Na-
ture intended us to do this and has
made us strong and given us an en-
durance not possessed by men in order
that we shall be able to bring our chil-
dren to maturity even without the aid
at the often transitorv and sometimes
migratory male; look about you and
see the proof of this assertion in the
many women of your acquaintance who
have done—and done well—what wel-
farers say is impossible and seek to
make impossible by law. And remem-
‘ber, every one of these restrictive laws
creates a bureau of enforcement, paid
for out of your pocket; and a corps
of inspectors to poke their noses into
your home and your way of providing
for and bringing up your children—
and you are taxed to pay the wages
of these inspectors and commissioners
and clerks, although your earning
power has been curtailed, often lost,
through the laws enforced bv them.
The women in Missouri and Indiana
have just killed in committee eight-
hour laws of this character; the women
in Illinois are fighting to kill one, as
are the women in Iowa; in Ohio they
have scotched the minimum wage law
by creating an investigation commis-
sion; in New York we expect to do the
same; in New Jersey the no-night-work
law, despite the excellent work of the
women to be affected by it, passed both
houses, but was amended to “take effect
December, 1924,”—a long interval in
which we hope to bring forward such
arguments, at the ballot box in Novem-
ber, 1923 and 1924, and in women’s
clubs and the newspapers by educa-
tional matter and speeches, that it
never will go into effect.
Remember this: after years of “‘pro-
tective” legislation for women and min-
ors the wages of women are at this
time so far below those of men, for
the same kind of work, that welfarers
seriously argue the necessity of a min-
imum wage law for women to raise
the level of wages, although the high-
est award ever made by a Minimum
Wage Board is $16.50! Instead of pro-
tecting the working woman, statutory
discrimination tends to harm her. The
worst thing one can do is to place
any handicaps on the economic inde-
pendence of a person. When women
and children are tied together by legis-
lation. their wage rates reflect that tie.
Giving women industrial equality
will not disrupt home life; rather will
it act as a check on the frequently over-
arrogant male who, controlling the
pursestrings and, through “protective”
laws for women, access to a pay en-
velope, ruins home life by his dictum
“Tf you don’t do as I say, I won't give
you the money for Johnny’s shoes—
or the grocery bill!”

Save us—and your daughters—from
this domination of our most sacred in-
stincts by making the path to earning
free from sentimental handicaps.

NEW JERSEY WOMEN WORKERS
RESENT STEVENS NO-NIGHT-
WORK BILL
The wage-earning women of New
Jersey appeared in force before the
Senate hearing on the Stevens bill pro-
hibiting night shift work to adult wom-
en, held at Trenton the afternoon of
January 29, and told the committee that
the proposed bill was in no sense a
protection but was expressly designed to
deprive them of work, although nearly
all of the 200 women present were
mothers. or had other dependents.
Those women who. have a vital inter-
est in the Stevens bill noted with amaze-
ment that the principal speaker for the
bill, Mrs. Maud Swartz, president of
the National Women’s Trade’ Union
League (who was once a_ working
woman but has for the past ten years
been solely engaged in promulgating
legislation designed to throw women out
of work or to limit their earning pow-
er), collapsed in the middle of a speech
purporting to show that women work-
ing—for pay!—on night shifts must of
necessity be unhealthy or immoral or
both. After her recovery, Mrs. Swartz
admitted that she had never worked on
a night shift—thus proving on two
counts that she did not know what she
was talking about; but she did not ad-
mit that her organization was insincere
in its stand against night work by tell-
ing the committee that the New York
vice-president of her League was work-
ing on a night shift on The New York
World, that she had chosen this shift
when, in 1921, women printers, after
eight years of legislative work and at a
total cost of $10,000, secured an amend-
ment to the New York State 54-hour and
no-night-work law permitting them to
go back to work at the usual hours of
their trade, and that during this entire
period the .Women’s Trade Union
League fought their efforts every year,
trying to tire and starve them out—yet
their vice-president was almost the first
to secure a $6l-a-week job on the night
shift after others had done all the work!
Dr. Harris of New York spoke for
the bill also, but all his arguments ap
plied to men as well as to women, “re-
gardless of sex” being one of his oft-
repeated sentence endings. Mrs. Feick-
ert, a Republican and wife of a banker;
Mrs. Norton, a Democrat but not a
working woman, and Assemblyman
Katherine Brown (Democrat) spoke for
the hill The latter were too sensible

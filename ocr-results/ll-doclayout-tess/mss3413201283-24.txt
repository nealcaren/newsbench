truth about Washington.
As a historian unswayed
by emotion and kindred
feelings, he has gone be-
Bind the romance which has so com-
pletely enveloped the character of our
first President and drawn a picture of
the man that has been altogether too
realistic for those worshippers who Ike
halos with thelr heroes in history.

As of the man, so of his movements
has a deal of untruth been uttered.
Rhode Island has contributed tts share.
-\ll over the State, as all up and down the
Atlantic coast, are houses in which
it is valiantly asserted Washington once
slept. Wherever one finds a house with
the date of erection preceding the
Revolution, one may confidently expect
to hear that at such and such a time
Washington was there and stayed through
the night.

But the facts do not make him out
such a continueus and persistent sleeper.
When the mists of tradition are brushed
away, the record shows that there were
just four places in the State, in which it
is certain that Washington spent some
time as a guest. Three were in Provi-
dence and,one in East Greenwich. All
claims of the rest to fame are of
dubious foundation.

Although Washington stayed several
days on each of his visits to Newport,
there is nothing to indicate where he
rested o’ night. That he spread his
time as generously and as conveniently
as he was able among several of the
leading families there is little question.
But neither he nor contemporary writers
Sive any names; and to-day in Newport
only Trinity Church claims him as’ its
own.

Whether or not he went to church
while in the town by the sea is a prob-
lem that may never be solved satis-
factorily. The tradition of his visit to
Trinity and of the pew he occupied has
been paraded and then seemingly punc-
tured so many times that any arbitrary
attempt to settle it would revive a con-
troversy that is better quiescent. li
may be said in all fairness, however
that the modern demand for hi8torical
fact regardless of romance has had its
effect in the case of old Trinity and its
cherished memory of the past.

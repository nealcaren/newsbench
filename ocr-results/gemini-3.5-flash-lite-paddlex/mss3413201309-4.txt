A man may hold property and pay
taxes in a hundred different towns
and yet can vote in only one.
The one where he votes may be the
one in which he does not pay taxes.
If voting and taxpaying went to-
gether, a man should have a vote in
every community where he paid taxes.
A system of government in which
the vote was based on property would
give the rich a power over the poor
that would destroy democratic govern-
ment.

WICKERSHAM

(Former Attorney-General of the U. S.)

The American man in time of peace
is an easy-going, trouble-avoiding
creature. Especially in his dealings
with women, he seems to have laid to
heart Shakespeare's admonition:
"In peace there's nothing so becomes
a man,
As meekness and humility."

The common answer given by the
average man who declares himself
in favor of woman suffrage is, "Oh,
well, the women want it and we might
as well let them have it." Whether
or not it is good for them; or still
more important, whether or not it is
to the interest of the commonwealth
that our already complex electorate
should be further complicated by re-
male suffrage, is brushed aside as a
matter of small moment. Yet I seri-
ously question whether anything like
a majority of the men of any Ameri-
can community are in favor of giving
women the vote. There are many
reasons against it. Some are obvious.
It is difficult to discuss the question
frankly. Most men feel a hesitancy
in discussing in a mixed assemblage
the fundamental questions of sex that
radically differentiate women from
men. The divine privilege of mother-
hood would seem to me to be of far
higher import than the vote, and I am
one of those who still think of women
as the object of love, adoration, of
reverence, whether as sweethearts,
wives or mothers, and therefore, the
thought of them engaged in elec-
tioneering, haranguing crowds from
wagons or soap boxes as one sees
many women nowadays, is repugnant
to all my ideas of womanhood.

Despite the clamorous claims of the
advocates of votes for women, there is
no preponderance of evidence that the
communities in which they have se-
cured that privilege are any better for
it. No wise laws for the protection of
women and children have been enact-
ed in the suffrage States that are not
to be found on the statute books of
New York. It may be seriously ques-
tioned whether in States where they
have no vote women do not possess
more real influence for good than in
States where they have the suffrage.
Certainly no woman in this State can
with justice complain that its laws
or their administration discriminate
against her or the interests of her
sex.

At this point Mr. Wickersham read
a clipping from the New York Tribune
of April 16th:

Washington, April 15th.—The Na-
tional Woman Suffrage headquarters
gave out to-night a statement by and
for Helen Ring Robinson, a State Sen-
ator in Colorado, as follows:

"I have come to Washington on
private matters, but now that I am
here I want to make my protest
against this conscription proposal be-
fore it is too late.

"We voting women in the West
have been greatly disturbed over the

to hear that any considerable number
of them are seeking the vote. In France
a woman never seems to forget that
she is the first of all a woman, and
her mission in these later years
seems to have been to make France
and the world realize that next to be-
ing a fighting man, there is nothing
more glorious than womanhood, and
that the supreme glory consists in
being the mother of a son who gives
his life for his country. Cannot the
women of America emulate their sis-
ters beyond the sea and learn that
not by intruding upon man's estate
is their sex to be glorified, but rather
by making of their womanhood that
holy thing that for centuries has held
mankind in thraldom to them.

GOVERNMENT A MAN'S JOB.

(From an Address at Cooper Union,
New York, by Henry A. Wise
Wood.)

Where, three years ago, I said to
the woman who wished to take hold
of the wheel of the Ship of State,
"Yes!" now I say to her "No!" This,
from now on, is a man's job. Gentle-
ness, love and mercy are needed, but
they are no longer in place on the
bridge; there there is room only for
resolution and force.

Today the world's every Ship of
State is in distress; on the bridge,
and at every important station there
must be men in control. Women have
their part, but it is with the immense-
ly important work below decks.
Theirs, indubitably, is not the func-
tion of government, or direction in
the hour of danger.

This has suddenly become a mascu-
line age. It calls for the uttermost
assertion of the martial instincts of
mankind. To be strong is to prevail;
to be weak is to invite defeat, to suf-
fer national disaster, the woman with
the man.

The nation that in this emergency
permits women to usurp the govern-
mental functions of men will rue the
day that it exchanged masculine
strength for feminine weakness.

The determined stand that the suf-
frage leaders, one and all, have taken
to defeat our preparations for de-
fense and to compel us to accept,
in the guise of peace, degradation at
the hands of Germany is an augury
of where we as a nation will end if
the political power which these leaders
are trying to grasp is finally put into
their hands.

Never was there a more eloquent
confession of woman's inability to sup-
port the strains of a war council than
that so pathetically made by Miss
Rankin in the moment of national
crisis, when, in her agony, she
cried out, "I want to stand by my
country, but I cannot vote for war!"

And again, when on April 25th, in
reply to a demand from her consti-
tuents that she support the Presi-
dent's selective conscription meas-
ure passed but three days later by
an enormous majority, she wired:
"I have heard of no one in the House
who is supporting the President's
conscription bill. Do you wish me to
stand alone for this?"

at which women could vote the

COLORADO defeated Prohibition
by 50,000 after women had voted
nearly twenty years. It was finally
carried by only 10,000 majority.
CALIFORNIA in 1914 defeated Pro-
hibition by a majority of nearly 200,-
000. It is everywhere admitted that
the women voted overwhelmingly
against it. In 1916 two Prohibition
bills were submitted to the people—
one for complete Prohibition, the
other for a restrictive sale measure—
and both were defeated.
The saloon and dance hall situation
in San Francisco has long been no-
torious. Indeed it has become so
intolerable that a popular uprising in
February has affected an at least
temporary suppression of the most
flagrant offenders,—and this not by
the use of the ballot but by the force
of aroused Public Opinion.

A REFLECTION UPON MEN.
The demand for "Votes for Women"
casts an unjustifiable reflection upon
the men of Maine.
It carries with it the insinuation
that men have wilfully neglected to
protect the rights of women.
This insinuation is both unjust and
ungenerous.
The creed of the men of Maine, as
of all the civilized world, is "Women
and Children First," on land as well
as on sea.
In countless ways the laws of
Maine give precedence to women in
property rights and obligations.
In their recognition of the rights of
women, the laws of male-suffrage
Maine are far in advance of those
of any state where women enjoy the
franchise.
Every suffragist, by demanding the
vote, practically declares that the
fathers, husbands, sons and brothers
of Maine are not to be trusted by
their wives, mothers, sisters and
daughters.
Every opponent of woman suffrage
repudiates this baseless allegation.
Speaking for at least 80 per cent.
of the women of Maine, we ask the
men of Maine to believe that we have
not lost our faith in them. And we
ask them to assure us of that belief
by VOTING "NO" ON WOMAN SUF-
FRAGE Sept. 10.

Woman Suffrage wins only when
its opponents neglect to vote. Don't
fail to vote NO Sept. 10.

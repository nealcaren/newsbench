ARTHUR PONSONBY, M. P.

To the Editor of The Herald:

Our citizens have been favored this
season with four outstanding British
speakers on world affairs. Sir GHlbert
Murray, Norman Angell, Miss Margaret
Bondneld of the Macdonald government,
Sir Herbert Ames, former financial dl-
rector of the League of Nations, and
now, in addition, comes Arthur Ponson-
by, M. P. His book, "Now is the Time,"
has profoundly affected Englisn senti-
ment. At a great meeting in December
in Albert hall, London, which holds 10,-
000 people, it was announced that 100,-
000 persons had sisned the Ponsonby
pledge “not to support or render war
service to any government which re-
sorts to arms." In a country which is
in the League of Nations and in the
World Court and under solemn pledges
to keep the peace with its 55 member
nations, and in which public sentiment
has gone much further toward world-
co-operation than it has here, Mr. Pon-
sonby can effectively urge measures
which at present would be imeftective
here. He finds us a land which has
increased its cost for defense over 600
times, while its population has -
creased 28 times, and which has mot
one single arbitration treaty that is
mot rendered abortive on any serious
issue by the exemption of matters
volving “vital interest and honor"

Mr. Ponsonby is not a mere enthusi-
ast. He is a seasoned statesman, whose
experience in Parliament and in the
sovernment has taught him the many
obstacles to be overcome. He declares
Iwould neither vote nor advocate the
abolition of the standing army and the
scrapping of the navy. Publie opinion
is not ready for such a step. No greater
error could be made than to present
them with the ultimate and concluding
stage of our policy when up till now we
have failed to convince them and draw
them through the preliminary stages,"
He has no patience with the “ansemic
and rather inefficient peace propaganda
which has been carried on in quoting
scripture texts and wasting breath in
vain attempts to convert the “authori-
ties" who have “made it their business
to deceive the people." He wastes no
time in telling unimaginative people
about the collossal figures of war cost
and of the unspeakable agony of mil-
lions. Comfortable, well-fed people,
more interested in football and movies
than in anything. else, will give little
thought to cause and effect and pos-
sible future tragedy due to their apathy
and stupid negligence. Mr. Ponsonby
demands virile rational efforts where
they will count. He aims to convert the
people who can still remember the loath-
someness and fiendishness of wholesale
human slaughter and show the futility
of it ere they pass on and a new genera-
tion that will see war under a glamor
of glory has been born.

He emphasizes the fact that, though
disputes occur all the time, they event-
nate in war generally only from 20 to
40 years. Between times, men are too
war-weary or too poor and weak to risk
a great war, and they settle their dis-

putes some other way. He finds far
more causes of international friction in
the last seven years than in the seven
years preceding 1914, but no great na-
tion has felt equal to renewing the mon-
strous destruction of innocent men by
innocent men. Now in time of peace,
when memories of soldiers are still vivid
and men are learning what the new de-
vices for wholesale slaughter can do in
turning to corpses in three hours 8,000,-
000 Londoners, is the time to create and
perfect the mechanism of government
and the sanity of minds that will show
the futiiy of settlement of boundary
lines or concessions of subsoil treasures
by explosives. The futility of war, and
the misunderstanding, secrecy and lies
that lead up to it in diplomatic chan-
nels, rather than cost and bloodshed,
are Mr. Ponsonby's line of attack. The
hoary fallacy that progress results from
the destruction of one's own species, in-
stead of from invention, discovery, edu-
cation, migration and sanitation, all this
Mr. Ponsonby eloquently proclaims. At
the meetings in Boston on Sunday and
Monday next Bostonians will have an
opportunity to hear a preacher of polit-
cal sanity. LUCIA AMES MBAD.

Mitchell of the “Old Sun"

(From the Sprinsficld Republican)

In the days when Charles A. Dana,
editor of the “old Sun," was becoming
less active,, it was still the habit of
the o am constituency to
credit to Mr. Dana the authorship of
every article of exceptional point or
brilliance that was printed on the pa-
per's editorial page. Mr. Dana once tried
to dispel that illusion. Asked if he had
not written certain notable articles, he
“No, they were written by
one of my bright young men—Mitchell.
1 wish I could write like that!"

Hdward P. Mitchell has just died in
his 75th year. Por half a century he
was “with the Sun"—old and new. And
there his heart was. 'The “old Sun" was
his life; and it must have been a joy
for a literary artist to work on it in
the days when Mr. Dana was far less
concerned about beating his morning
competitors with the news than with
making his editorial page crackle and
bite, and the “stories" of his reporters
read like a Stevenson romance or the
Kipling swagger.

Mr. Mitchell as an editorial writer
over a long series of years contributed
very much of the most original, salty
and fronically humorous matter that
gave to the Sun its pecullar and inimi-
table distinction. The Republican was
often the butt of his fun making and
satirical word coinage, particularly in
the days of political mugwumpery and
the late Charles Goodrich Whiting's
finest poetic prose in Nature editorials.
But it was all done without malice and
was rich in an underlying sympathy, it
feathered and barbed with a piercing
and contrary point of view.

Anyone who reads Mr. Mitchell's pub-
lished autobiography .and reminiscences
discerns at once his gift as a writer of
unusual excellence. Was he "wasted"
on the anonymous editorial to which
his life work was devoted? Not wasted,
if an impersonal test of accomplishment
be applied. If his talent was best ex-
ercised as a rapid and shrewd com-
mentator on the changing scene that
daily journallsm brings constantly inte
view, then he was cast where he be-
longed. Personal fame? Those who
want it may seek it. Mitchell was one
who could size it up.

By MARIE TROMMER

No one but a man deeply sensitive
and possessing a soul responding to
every cry of pain, no matter how
distant, could reproduce the horrors
of a pogrom without actually hay-
ing witnessed one. It was Benjamin
Ovryn, the young Jewish artist, who
thus succeeded in depicting a mas-
sacre in Kolomea, Poland, as though
he himself had been there at the
time and lived through all the hor-
Tors.

Ovryn was born in Kiey, South
Russia, and received his first art
training at the Kiev Art School, con-
tinuing his studies after his arrival
in this country a few years ago, at
the National Academy of Design. He
made his American debut last
Spring at the Independent Artists
Exhibition, with a few landscapes of
the Hudson River and rural scenes,
ably portraying their ever-changing
coloring and picturesqueness.

Ovryn's two pogrom paintings
were exhibited at the Independent
Artists' Show of this Spring, which
closed on Sunday, March 18th. Both
canvases are striking in their orig-
inality of conception and heartren-
dering realism. Two Jews just
killed by the savage mob and a
policeman watching indifferently the
terrible massacre, is the theme of
one of the paintings, The other
shows some mutilated flesh that was
a body, possibly only a few minutes
previous.

Yet it would be wrong to think
that Ovryn's art is morbid—he is
too versatile for that, as is best
borne out by his paintings of trees
and other cheerful and peaceful
subjects, expressive compositions,
strong in line and color, exhibited
last Fall at the Salons of America,
where he is also going to exhibit
thia nmwine

ror," a painting in which his artis-
tic soul will find full self-expression.
The young artist, however, is far
from concentrating on himself alone
—he believes in helping other
aspirants and directs, free of
the School of Painting of the Y. M.
and W. H. A. in Williamsbursg,
Brooklyn, devoting his talent and
time to young Jewish embryo artists
who may not have the opportunity
or means to study art elsewhere.

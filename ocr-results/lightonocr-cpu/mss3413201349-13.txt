"Brother husbands"——

It was the voice of Lady Astor's hus-
band crying out to other husbands "in
the wilderness of women" at the Pan-
American Conference. Lord Astor had
come reluctantly to the platform after
his lady had finished speaking at the
massmeeting at the Century Roof.

"Brother husbands," his Lordship re-
peated.

He got no further. The voice of his
Lordship was drowned completely in the
feminine applause. But he stood his
ground on the platform, for, far back
in the audience, he saw the heads of a
few "brother husbands," and he had a
message for them. Then, too, Lady
"Nancy" was still on the platform
watching her husband. He knew that
he must "make good."

So he began a third time:
"Brother husbands, a word of assur-
ance of good hope"——

# Applause Wins Again.

Again handelapping overwhelmed him. Lady Astor’s husband, however, was determined to speak. Lady Astor was still standing by with a most “impossible look” in her eye and seeming to enjoy the occasion hugely. And there were the few “brother husbands” looking to him eagerly, encouragingly.

“Brother husbands,” said his Lordship as the room grew quiet. “I have been hanging around the ‘outskirts’ of this conference for several days. And I have noticed a number of extremely nervous men.”

Instantly every man present had a kindly feeling for Lady Astor’s husband. There was a bond of sympathy between them. Instantly, every woman conjured up visions of her husband and other delegates’ husbands wandering around the edges of the convention halls late at night, waiting for their wives. They recalled the voice of one husband to another down on the street the night of the banquet, saying plaintively, dejectedly:

“I guess the day of man is passed. Women are in politics. Our homes are broken up and we must shuffle along. There’s no use trying any more. Come and—”

But what was this Lady Astor’s husband was saying?

## Afraid For Their Wives.

“These men are afraid for their wives to take an interest in politics. They are afraid their homes will be broken up, that they will be forgotten and their children neglected.

“Yes, yes,” thought each wife. “That’s just what my husband says.”

“That’s right, that’s right,” mentally agreed each man.

“Brother husbands, if our wives take a real interest in public life, we need to have no fear for our homes. It’s all easy, if, like me, you have the right wife,” the speaker declared.

And then Lord Astor said a few words that made some of the men and women think.

He recalled the woman who is not in politics, of the thousands who play bridge or give too much attention to dress or clubs, and reminded them that “brother husbands” that some of these women scarcely knew their children by sight and frequently were a bit hazy about their husbands.

“Brother husbands, think big,” said Lord Astor soberly. “Don’t use a pocket dictionary to find your definition of ‘home.’”

## VISCOUNT ASTOR SPEAKS

---

Addresses “Brother Husbands;” Mrs. Catt Defends Activity Of Women Voters.

Storms of applause greeted Lady Nancy Astor last night when she pleaded for America's participation in the League of Nations at a press-meeting that packed the Century Roof and an overflow meeting that packed the Hotel Belvedere ballroom with women from every country in all the Americas and from every State in the Union.

But she checked the demonstration sharply each time.

"Hand-clapping won't get it," she said. "That takes work—and votes."

Her address was the feature of both gatherings; and at both there were calls for a speech from her husband, Lord Astor, who made friendly talks addressed to his "brother husbands" in the audiences, assuring them that the activity of women in political life is not necessarily dangerous to the home or to the welfare of the men.

At both meetings, likewise, addresses were made by Dr. Charles E. Merriam, professor of political economy in the University of Chicago, who urged the scientific study of political problems and popular reactions to political appeals; by Mrs. Carrie Chapman Catt, president of the International Woman Suffrage Alliance, who defended the present activities of the League of Women Voters; and by Mrs. Sumner T. McKnight, treasurer of the League, who made an effective appeal for funds for the organization.

Receives Bouquet Of Roses.

Lady Astor, before leaving the stage, received a huge bouquet of Richmond roses from the Virginia economy to the convention. It was presented by Miss Adele Clark, president of the Virginia Association for Woman Suffrage, who congratulated Lady Astor and Virginia upon the fact that the Old Dominion, which left the mother country because it could have no representation in Parliament, now had one of her own daughters in that body.

Notice had been given that the Century Roof would be opened at 7 o'clock. Half an hour before that time the halls leading to the elevators were packed with women anxious to enter. Seats had been reserved for the convention delegates and alternates. All the other seats were quickly filled and the crowd below became more dense than ever. Two hundred delegates were unable to gain admittance. Word of this having been sent to the roof, request was made that a like number of Baltimore women leave the hall and go to the Belvedere for an overflow meeting. The promise was made that all the speakers would go to the hotel ballroom. After a moment of hesitation the required number of women left the roof and in time the police managed to clear the hallways.

Search for Missing Civil Rights Law
for Nation's Capital Gets Under Way
Five-Point Action Plan Set Up by Committee
of Citizens; Group Weary of Probe Stall

Representatives from 25 District 
organizations met last Thursday
 night to chart a course in
search of the District's "missing
Civil Rights Law."
  Citizens of the District were
made aware of the "lost clause"
by the public issuance of the report 
on "Segregation in Washington"
 by the National Committee
on Segregation in the Nation's
Capital during Nov., 1948.
  Following the uncovering of
this anti-discrimination law, passed 
by the District Legislative Assembly 
of 1873, the D.C. chapter of
the National Lawyers Guild pre
pared a brief in which an opinion
was given that they were still valid.
  Promised an Opinion
  During May of this year, the 
District Commissioners announced
that the Corporation Counsel's office 
was "studying" the lawyers'
guild brief and promised to issue 
an opinion after the investigation
is complete.
  John B. Duncan, chairman of 
the special citizens committee for
enforcement of the D.C. anti-discrimination
 law, has grown weary
of the corporation counsel's "investigation."
  Duncan says: "It is a foregone
conclusion that all those interests
in our city that have found segregation
 financially profitable will
want to keep this thing perennially 
'under study.'"
  The lawyers' guild, whose forcing
 of the "lost cause" issue has 
caused considerable "head scratching" 
in the corporation counsel
office, content that the civil
rights law is still enforceable.
  Still Are Law, Guild Says
  In support of this opinion the
guild says:
  "The 1872 act and a similar
measure enacted in 1873 still are
the law of the District of Columbia.
  "Some people thought that just
because some of the old laws were
not written down in the latest 
legal code, they couldn't be enforced 
any longer.
  "But this was not the case.  In
1901, Congress adopted a code
of laws for the nation's capital.
It repealed man of the acts of
the Legislative Assembly.
  "But it specifically declared that
'police regulations' and all acts
and parts of acts relating to muni-
cipal affairs only' would remain
in force.
  "One man who was arrested for
mistreating his horse hired a lawyer
 to fight the case.  The lawyer
said his client could not be fined
under the old law, because it 
had been passed by the Legislative 
Assembly and was now omit-
ted from the written code.
  Old Law Upheld
"The United States Court of Appeals
 in 1908 upheld the conviction
and ruled the law was still in
force."
  The committee seeking for the
enforcement of the anti-discrimination
 "lost clause" has planned
that:
  1. A delegation of civic leaders
arranged a conference with the
District Commissioners to establish
 a timetable for the enforcement
 of these laws.
  2. A campaign be undertaken
to popularize the "missing laws"
and to institute widespread discussion
 through radio, press, public
 debates and meetings.
  3. Organizations, clubs, churches, 
etc. be provided speakers, discussion
 leaders and printed material 
on the laws.
  4. The committee act as a coordinating
 body for pooling information
 or for any joint effort
agreed upon.
  5. The support of all organizations 
and individuals concerned
with the problems of segregation
in the capital be enlisted.
  Long Forgotten Words
  The actual wording of the civil
rights law, long forgotten, reads:
  That any restaurant keeper, propraetor,
 any hotel keeper or proprietor, 
or keepers of ice-cream saloons
or places where soda-water is kept
for sale, or keepers of barber shops
and bathing houses, refusing to sell
or wait upon any respectable well-behaved
 person, without regard to 
race, color, or previous servitude,
shall be guilty of a misdemeanor,
and upon conviction in court, shall
be fined $100 and shall forfeit his or
her license as keeper or owner of a
restaurant, hotel, ice-cream saloon,
or soda fountain, as the case may be.
  It shall not be lawful for the Register 
or any officer of the District of 
Columbia to issue a license to any 
person or persons, or to their agent
or agents, who shall have forfeited
their license under the provisions of
this act, until a period of one year
shall have elapsed after such forfeiture.

GOP Can't take Credit
For Ending Café J.C. Afro June 20 - [23?]
  The attempt of the present Republican administration to take credit for the abolishment of segregation in Washington's restaurants drew sharp rebuttal this week from principals who had been
fighting the issue since 1950.  Mr. Eisenhower in a speech at Mount Rushmore, S. D., brought the
issue to a head when he credited the Supreme Court decision to successful appeal from a lower
court decision made by the present Attorney General, Herbert Brownell Jr.

Mrs. Terrell Says
WASHINGTON
  Mrs. Mary Church Terrell took 
issue Friday with reports that 
the Eisenhower administration 
is "taking credit" for ending
segregation in D.C. restaurants.
She said:
  "I am greatly opposed to the
Eisenhower administration taking
 credit for opening the res-
taurants.  It had nothing to do
with it.
  "It was the Co-ordinating
Committee for the Enforcement
of the Anti-Discrimination Laws
in D.C. that did it - the members
 of the committee and their
attorneys.
  Interracial Committee
  "I am happy to say," Mrs.
Terrell added, "that the committee
 is composed of both colored
 and white members and I
want to say how loyal and hard
working they all were.
  "I'd like to mention especially 
Mrs. Annie Stein, our former
chairman, and our attorneys,
Joseph Forer and David Rein."
  News dispatches Friday morning
 on President Dwight D. Eisenhower's
 speech at Mt. Rushmore
 National Memorial in
South Dakota, quoted him as
citing the end of segregation in
D.C. eating-places as one of the
achievements of this administration.

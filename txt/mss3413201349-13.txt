THE EVENING SUN, BALTIMORE. THURSDAY, APRIL 27, 1922.
Lord Astor Speaks Words 
Of Comfort to Husbands
Declares Wives In Public Life Will Not Break Up 
Homes If They Are The Right 
Kind of Wives. 
"Brother husbands -"
It was the voice of Lady Astor's husband 
crying out to other husbands "in 
the wilderness of women" at the Pan-American 
Conference. Lord Astor had 
come reluctantly to the platform after 
his lady had finished speaking at the 
massmeeting at the Century Roof. 
"Brother husbands," his Lordship repeated. 
He got no further. The voice of his 
Lordship was drowned completely in the 
feminine applause. But he stood his
 ground on the platform, for, far back 
in the audience, he saw the heads of a
 few "brother husbands," and he had a 
message for them. Then, too, Lady 
"Nancy" was still on the platform 
watching her husband. He knew that 
he must "make good."
So he began a third time:
"Brother husbands, a word of assurance, 
of good hope" - 
Applause Wins Again.
Again handclapping overwhelmed him. 
Lady Astor's husband, however, was 
determined to speak. Lady Astor was 
still standing by with a most "impish 
look" in her eye and seeming to enjoy 
the occasion hugely. And there were 
the few "brother husbands" looking to 
him eagerly, encouragingly. 
"Brother husbands," said his Lordship 
as the room grew quiet. "I have 
been hanging around the 'outskirts' of 
this conference for several days. And 
I have noticed a number of extremely 
nervous men."
Instantly every man present had a 
kindly feeling for Lady Astor's husband. 
There was a bond of sympathy between 
them. Instantly, every woman conjured 
up visions of her husband and other 
delegates' husbands wandering around 
the edges of the convention halls late at 
night, waiting for their wives. They 
recalled the voice of one husband to 
another down on the street the night of 
the banquet, saying plaintively, dejectedly:
"I guess the day of man is passed. 
Women are in politics. Our homes are 
broken  up and we must shuffle along.
There's no use trying any more. Come 
on, old scout, let's park near the curb 
and - "
But what was this Lady Astor's husband 
was saying?
Afraid For Their Wives. 
"These men are afraid for their wives 
to take an interest in politics. They are 
afraid their homes will be broken up, 
that they will be forgotten and their 
children neglected.
"Yes, yes," thought each wife. "That's 
just what my husband says."
"That's right, that's right," mentally 
agreed each man.
"Brother husbands, if our wives take 
a real interest in public life, we need 
have no fear for our homes. It's all 
easy, if, like me, you have the right 
wife," the speaker declared. 
And then Lord Astor said a few words 
that made some of the men and women 
think. 
He recalled the woman who is not in 
politics, of the thousands who play 
bridge or give too much attention to 
dress or clubs, and reminded his "brother
husbands" that some of these women 
scarcely knew their children by sight 
and frequently were a bit hazy about 
their husbands. 
"Brother husbands, think big," said 
Lord Astor soberly. "Don't use a pocket
dictionary to find your definition of
'home'."
Lord and Lady Astor seem to be very 
"much in love with each other." As 
far as appearances go, they are admirably 
suited. Lord Astor is tall and 
slender and dark, while Lady Astor is 
small and blonde. They both talk a 
good deal about their children and home. 
Lord Astor seems to serve as a sort of 
"steadying" influence over the buoyant, 
free spirited "Nancy."
Full Program Today. 
Lady Astor has a full program of 
teas, receptions and visiting for today. 
She is resting at Mrs. John W. Garrett's 
all the morning and with the Lord 
Astor will go to visit the Evergreen 
Institute for the Blind. 
From there Lady Astor will go to 
the luncheon of the Daughters of the 
American Revolution. At 4:30 o'clock 
this afternoon Mrs. Carrie Chapman 
Catt, honorary president of the National 
League and president of the International 
Suffrage Alliance, will be 
"at home" to Lady Astor and the Canadian 
delegates to the Pan-American 
Conference at the Belvedere. 
At 5 o'clock the Canadian delegates 
will have tea for Lady Astor at the 
Baltimore Country Club and tonight 
Lord and Lady Astor will be the guests 

of honor at a dinner given by Mrs. John 
W. Garrett. 
Will Leave Tomorrow. 
The Astor party will leave for Washington 
tomorrow, where they will stay 
with Mrs. Gifford Pinchot. 
Senator Swanson, of Virginia, will 
give a luncheon for the members of the 
party tomorrow, and at 3.30 o'clock the 
Press Club in Washington will hold a 
reception for Lord and Lady Astor.
Monday Lady Astor will go to Philadelphia 
to speak to the Trade Union 
League and will proceed to Virginia 
next Tuesday.

VISCOUNT ASTOR SPEAKS
Addresses "Brother Husbands;" 
Mrs. Catt Defends Activity 
Of Women Voters. 
Storms of applause greeted Lady 
Nancy Astor last night when she 
pleaded for American participation
in the League of Nationals at a mass-meeting 
that packed the Century Roof 
and an overflow meeting that packed 
the Hotel Belvedere ballroom with 
women from every country in all the 
Americas and from every State in the 
Union. 
But she checked the demonstration 
sharply each time. 
"Hand-clapping won't get it," she 
said. "That takes work - and votes."
Her address was the feature of both 
gatherings; and at both there were 
calls for a speech from her husband, 
Lord Astor, who made friendly talks
addressed to his "brother husbands" 
in the audiences, assuring them that 
the activity of women in political life 
is not necessarily dangerous to the 
home or the welfare of the men. 
At both meetings, likewise, addresses 
were made by Dr. Charles E.
Merriam, professor of political 
economy in the University of Chicago, 
who urged the scientific study 
of political problems and popular 
reactions to political appeals; by 
Mrs. Carrie Chapman Catt, president 
of the International Woman Suffrage 
Alliance, who defended the 
present activities of the League of 
Women Voters; and by Mrs. Sumner 
T. McKnight, treasurer of the 
League, who made an effective appeal 
for funds for the organization. 
Receives Bouquet of Roses. 
Lady Astor, before leaving the 
stage, received a huge bouquet of 
Richmond roses from the Virginia 
delegation of the convention. It was 
presented by Miss Adele Clark, president
of the Virginia Association for 
Woman Suffrage, who congratulated 
Lady Astor and Virginia upon the 
fact that the Old Dominion, which 
left the mother country because it 
could have no representation in Parliament, 
now had one of her own 
daughters in that body. 
Notice had been given that the Century 
Roof would be opened at 7 
o'clock. Half an hour before that 
time the halls leading to the elevators 
were packed with woman anxious to 
enter. Seats had been reserved for 
the convention delegates and alternates.
All the other seats were 
quickly filled and the crowd below became 
more dense than ever. Two 
hundred delegates were unable to gain 
admittance. Word of this having 
been sent to the roof, request was 
made that a like number of Baltimore 
women leave the hall and go to 
the Belvedere for an overflow meeting. 
The promise was made that all 
the speakers would go to the hotel 
ballroom. After a moment of hesitation 
the required number of women 
left the roof and in time the police 
managed to clear the hallways. 
Lady Astor, in both her addresses, 
digressed at times to elaborate point 
which she wishes to emphasize. She 
said in part:
Text of Lady Astor's Address.

the House of Commons I may tell 
you frankly I didn't half realize what 
the voyage would be like. I soon discovered 
that it was the kind of voyage 
which would take the spiritual 
faith of the Pilgrim fathers and the 
courage of Sir Francis Drake. As 
they both sailed from my port, perhaps 
they remembered me; anyhow,

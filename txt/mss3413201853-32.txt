Not Easily Aroused
For 25 years, a few women in this
Oriental country have been trying 
to sound a tocsin that would arouse
these Filipinas, as the Spanish call
them, to a responsibility larger than
the one they bear so capably in
their homes. It was Carrie Chapman
Catt, visiting in Manila in 1912, 
who first pointed the way to a 
handful of hopeful but frightened
Filipino women.
The mass of women did not want
"to step into the mire of politics";
plied those awesome "people" by 
millions; made them people of all
the world gazing with curious eyes 
upon the Philippine Islands, waiting
like judges to see what the
Filipino woman would do with the
opportunity offered her on Apr. 30
to emancipate herself.
According to Mrs. Lim, people
will talk about whatever women do.
The Island women must choose between
being called "forward" or
"backward." The question answered
so emphatically on Friday the 30th 
by 460,000 women was, should they
"keep their place within the house"
and so avoid being styled "forward"
here at home, or step out to
help men shape this newly formed 
government and so escape being
called "backward" by all the world.
Leader Described
There is no better woman in this
chain of islands than Pilar Hidalgo 
Lim to strike into life a world consciousness
among her sisters.
"Dimpled Pilar," as her friends call
her, is a pure Philippine product.
Because, like an average girl in this
tropic land, she was brought up on
a cocoanut farm with three sisters
and four brothers, educated in the

six children she has the confidence

emergence from Spanish rule,
young enough to be flexible in her
opinions. She not only knows but

Wears Native Dress
The old-timers, both men and
women, like Mrs. Lim because she
does not affect European or States
dress. She is always perfectly 
gowned in the delicate pineapple
cloth of her own land. And they
like her because of her six children,
youngest 8 months, whom she does
not neglect even while taking into
her heart all the children of these
Islands in her attempt to secure for 
them better legislation.
They like her because she and
General Vicente Lim, first Filipino 
to be graduated from West Point,
have established for themselves a
happy family life.
Their courtship started among the
pines of mile-high Baguio when
Lim was a lieutenant in the United
States Army. The meeting was
Hollywood stuff: Pilar, returning
from a mountain hike with her
sisters, was resting beside the road.
The young West Pointer drove by,
offered the girls a ride...That was 
April. In August they were married
without ever having been introduced
properly, much to the chagrin
of Pilar's very strict mother.
Jolo, far south in the Sulu Sea was
their first post, down among the
dangerous Moros.
After the third baby came, they
were transferred to the States
where, between 1926 and 1929, they
were stationed at Fort Bening, 
Georgia, Fort Leavenworth, Kansas, 
and the War College in Washington,
D. C. Those are years when
Mrs. Lim made her first appearances
as a speaker, telling army
wives and women's clubs about the
life of a Filipino woman, exhibiting 
to them the intricacies of Filipino
dress.
American Ideas
In Georgia, her greatest ambition 
was to have a permanent wave, but
lest she be thought a mulatto,
Major Lim persuaded her to wait
until she was ready to sail for
Manila. She is full of amusing

when she was thought to he Japanese,
Chinese, Siamese--anything
oriental but what she is--four years

It is those four years which gave

Author Tells Experiences
Starting as a tourist, but ending
as an assistant campaigner, I went
on a tour of the northern provinces
of the Island of Luzon with Mrs.
Lim and her companion, Mrs.
Josefa Escoda, the "right-hand-man" 
in this suffrage work. Traveling 
by automobile, we made from 
four to eight appearances a day.
Every town, no matter how small,
had erected a bamboo "Welcome"
arch decorated with flowers and [in]
one town lace which had obviously
been borrowed from the altar. Always
there were bouquets, refreshments,
and the most gracious [of]
speeches.
Never were there enough chairs
for the meeting held in the Plaza
or Municipal Building. The entire
feminine population was out and,
crowding around the edges, most
of the men! And it was the men
who voiced the loudest approval.
One town president, so impressed
with Mrs. Lim's knowledge, understanding
and authority, said that
he could see in her a future president
of the Philippine Islands' Commonwealth, 
at which women
cheered themselves hoarse. Such
masculine recognition of their No.
1 woman was not expected.
Now serving a second term as
national president of the Federation
of Women's Clubs, she is already
well known throughout the Islands 
and genuinely loved because she 
will sit down and talk about unsatisfied
predilections for dramatics,
about raising children and chickens, 
about getting a Ph.D. in Math.
(which she taught at the University
of the Philippines in Manila)
about being incurably romantic and
keeping the citadel of one's heart 
intact until the one man comes 
along, (she did, and recognized him
instantly) about roasting a pig on 
a spit, (lechon, they call it here) or 
winning a Senator to suffrage, 
about envying the American woman
who is permitted unchaperoned

Likes to Dance
She will quote international law
or Spanish poetry, as you will. She
does not play bridge, "because the
General is so expert at it!" She can 
sew, but she prefers to dance. Divorce
is against her religious principles,
for she is Catholic--as are 
80 out of every 100 in these Islands
--but she understands her time and
she is willing to accede to whatever
plan is finally deemed best by the
majority of Filipino women.
She wants her women to pass 
quickly through this evolutionary 
stage and take their place beside
the women of the world without
that slow torture suffered by the
American women as they shed their
Victorian attitudes in the days of 
the first typewriters and telephones.
Mrs. Lim speaks of the vote in
terms of responsibility to their
country and their children rather
than as a personal right for which
they should fight. She is looking
far down the years.
She sees her two-year-old nation
stepping out of its rompers and into
full-dress among the nations of the
world. She knows, better than
anyone else, how important is the
mother in the rearing of a Filipino 
and she is determined that her
country shall not go astray. It will
not if the women now so wide
awake will not slip back into prolonged
siestas.
"Our struggle," says Mrs. Lim, "is
not with our men. It is with our 
own indifference to our country's 
need, our own inertia. The cause
of suffrage in our Islands has not
become a battle of the sexes. Rather
it is a movement to place the Filipino 
woman in a position better to 
co-operate with the men in securing
what is best for the country as
a whole." She loses no chance to 
remind the women that this is a 
re-entry which they are making
into the field of politics, not an
entry. Centuries ago, before the
Spanish came, women ran this government,
sat in war councils, fought
in battles.
Cool-Tempered, Logical
During Spain's oppression, they 
exercised all their traditional
authority in their own homes and
in their small business enterprises
which always exist to contribute to 
the family budget. Now that the
cycle has swung full round, they
are ready once more to assume responsibility
for their country among
world powers.
Beside outstanding women of
other nations, Pilar Hidalgo Lim
will take her place. In beauty as 
rare as a Philippine orchid; cool-tempered,
logical, she is able to direct
her remarkable intellectual
force with all the subtlety of the
Oriental. Today, as votes far in 
excess of the required 300,000 pile
up, we get the whole world's answer 
to "what will people say?"; it
is, "Cheers for Mrs. Lim! More
power to Filipino women!"
